Intermediate Value Theorem

A continuous function cannot jump. If you draw its graph without lifting your pen, it must pass through every height between its starting value and its ending value. That intuition is the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT), one of the foundational guarantees of calculus.

Theorem (Intermediate Value Theorem). Let $f$ be continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$. If $k$ is any real number satisfying $f(a) \leq k \leq f(b)$ (or $f(b) \leq k \leq f(a)$), then there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f(c) = k$.

The proof relies on the completeness of the real numbers — the idea that there are no "gaps" on the number line — but the theorem's power lies in what you can do with it, not the proof machinery.

Worked example. Show that $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$ has a root in $(1, 2)$.

Compute the endpoints:

$$f(1) = 1 - 1 - 1 = -1 < 0$$ $$f(2) = 8 - 2 - 1 = 5 > 0$$

Since $f$ is a polynomial (continuous everywhere) and $0$ lies between $f(1) = -1$ and $f(2) = 5$, the IVT guarantees a $c \in (1, 2)$ with $f(c) = 0$. The root exists — even though we haven't found its exact value.

Problem-solving application. The IVT is the theoretical backbone of root-finding algorithms like bisection search. The strategy: if $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs, a root is trapped in $[a, b]$. Evaluate the midpoint $m = \frac{a+b}{2}$, then check which half still contains the sign change, and repeat. Each iteration halves the interval, so after $n$ steps the root is located to within $\frac{b-a}{2^n}$.

A key caution: the theorem requires continuity. The function $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ satisfies $f(-1) = -1$ and $f(1) = 1$, yet it never equals $0$ — because it has a discontinuity at $x = 0$, the IVT's hypothesis fails.

When applying IVT on an exam or in a proof, always verify three things: (1) $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, (2) the target value $k$ lies between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, and (3) your conclusion states existence, not uniqueness — the IVT guarantees at least one $c$, but there may be more.
