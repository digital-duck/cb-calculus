Differential Calculus

Definition. Let $f : (a, b) \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval. The derivative of $f$ at a point $x_0 \in (a, b)$ is defined as the limit

$$f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h},$$

provided this limit exists and is finite. When $f'(x_0)$ exists, we say $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$. Geometrically, $f'(x_0)$ equals the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(x_0, f(x_0))$. Analytically, it measures the instantaneous rate of change of $f$ with respect to $x$.

Worked Example. Let $f(x) = x^3 - 2x$. We compute $f'(1)$ directly from the definition:

$$f'(1) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(1+h)^3 - 2(1+h) - (1^3 - 2 \cdot 1)}{h}.$$

Expanding the numerator: $(1 + 3h + 3h^2 + h^3) - (2 + 2h) - (-1) = h + 3h^2 + h^3$. Dividing by $h$ and taking $h \to 0$ yields $f'(1) = 1$. The tangent line at $(1, -1)$ therefore has equation $y = x - 2$.

Key Theorem (Differentiability Implies Continuity). If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_0$.

Proof sketch. Write $f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0) = \dfrac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h} \cdot h$. As $h \to 0$, the first factor converges to $f'(x_0)$ and the second to $0$, so the product tends to $0$, giving $\lim_{h\to 0} f(x_0 + h) = f(x_0)$. $\square$

The converse fails: $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there, since the left- and right-hand limits of the difference quotient are $-1$ and $+1$, respectively.

Lab Cell (SymPy).

import sympy as sp

x = sp.Symbol('x')
f = x**3 - 2*x

df   = sp.diff(f, x)          # symbolic derivative: 3x² - 2
val  = df.subs(x, 1)          # evaluate at x=1: 1
line = val*(x - 1) + f.subs(x, 1)   # tangent line: x - 2

print("f'(x) =", df)
print("f'(1) =", val)
print("tangent at x=1:", line)
