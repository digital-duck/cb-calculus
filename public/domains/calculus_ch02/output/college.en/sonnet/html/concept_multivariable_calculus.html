Multivariable Calculus

Definition. Let $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function of $n$ variables. The partial derivative of $f$ with respect to the $i$-th variable $x_i$, evaluated at a point $\mathbf{a} = (a_1, \ldots, a_n)$, is defined as

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\mathbf{a}) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a_1, \ldots, a_i + h, \ldots, a_n) - f(\mathbf{a})}{h},$$

provided the limit exists. The gradient of $f$ is the vector field $\nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \ldots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right)$, which points in the direction of steepest ascent of $f$ at each point.

Worked Example. Let $f(x, y) = x^2 y + e^y \sin x$. To find $\nabla f$, treat each variable in turn while holding the other constant:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy + e^y \cos x, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + e^y \sin x.$$

At the point $(0, 0)$: $\nabla f(0,0) = (0 + 1 \cdot 1,\; 0 + 1 \cdot 0) = (1, 0)$. The function increases most steeply in the positive $x$-direction at the origin.

Key Theorem (Clairaut's Theorem). If $f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ has continuous second partial derivatives in a neighbourhood of $(a, b)$, then the mixed partials commute:

$$\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \, \partial y}(a,b) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \, \partial x}(a,b).$$

Proof sketch. Both mixed partials equal the same symmetric limit involving four-point differences of $f$; continuity guarantees the order of the limiting operations is interchangeable. $\square$

This symmetry is not merely algebraic convenience — it underlies the integrability conditions in differential geometry and the exactness criteria for differential forms.

Lab Cell (SymPy).

from sympy import symbols, exp, sin, diff

x, y = symbols('x y')
f = x**2 * y + exp(y) * sin(x)

f_xy = diff(f, x, y)
f_yx = diff(f, y, x)

print("f_xy =", f_xy)
print("f_yx =", f_yx)
print("Equal:", f_xy == f_yx)   # True — Clairaut confirmed

Running this cell prints identical expressions for both mixed partials, providing a computational verification of Clairaut's theorem for this particular function. Experimenting with other choices of $f$ builds intuition for when the hypothesis — continuity of second partials — is essential.
