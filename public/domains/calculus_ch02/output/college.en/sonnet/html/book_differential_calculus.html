Payoff

Differential calculus is the mathematics of instantaneous change. Where elementary algebra describes static relationships and arithmetic computes fixed quantities, differential calculus asks a fundamentally different question: how fast is a quantity changing at this exact moment? The answer, encoded in the derivative, transforms our ability to analyze, optimize, and predict the behavior of any system whose state evolves continuously.

The central object. Let $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a function. The derivative of $f$ at a point $a$ is defined as the limit

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h},$$

provided this limit exists. Geometrically, $f'(a)$ is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at $(a, f(a))$; analytically, it is the best linear approximation to $f$ near $a$.

Worked example. Consider a particle whose position is given by $s(t) = t^3 - 3t^2 + 2$ metres at time $t$ seconds. Its instantaneous velocity at $t = 2$ is

$$s'(2) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(2+h)^3 - 3(2+h)^2 + 2 - (8 - 12 + 2)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h^3 + 6h^2 + 12h - 12h - 6h^2}{h} \cdot \frac{1}{1}.$$

Applying the power rule directly: $s'(t) = 3t^2 - 6t$, so $s'(2) = 12 - 12 = 0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The particle is momentarily at rest — a fact invisible to algebra alone.

Key theorem (Fermat's criterion). If $f$ is differentiable on an open interval and attains a local extremum at $c$, then $f'(c) = 0$. This theorem converts the qualitative question "where does $f$ achieve its best value?" into the algebraic task of solving $f'(c) = 0$, making optimization tractable across science and engineering.

from sympy import symbols, diff, solve
t = symbols('t')
s = t**3 - 3*t**2 + 2
critical = solve(diff(s, t), t)
print(critical)          # [0, 2]

Every concept built across this textbook — limits, continuity, algebraic manipulation of polynomials and trigonometric functions — has been scaffolding toward this moment. The derivative unifies them into a single, powerful lens.

You now hold that lens. Turn it on the domain that matters most to you: whether that is the trajectory of a projectile, the marginal cost in an economic model, the rate of a chemical reaction, or the gradient that guides a neural network toward truth. Each is a chapter waiting to be written — and the first sentence of each begins with $\frac{d}{dx}$.
