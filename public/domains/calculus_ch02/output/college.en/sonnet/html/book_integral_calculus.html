Payoff

Integral calculus is the art of recovering a whole from its pieces. Where differential calculus asks "how fast is this changing at this instant?", integral calculus reverses the question: "if I know the rate of change everywhere, what is the total accumulation?" That reversal — captured precisely by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus — turns limits of sums into antiderivatives, and antiderivatives into a practical algorithm for computing areas, volumes, accumulated quantities, and probability masses.

The Fundamental Theorem states that if $F' = f$ on $[a, b]$, then

$$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = F(b) - F(a).$$

This is not merely a definition; it is the bridge between the local behavior of a function and its global effect. Every integral you compute by finding an antiderivative relies on this bridge.

A worked example. A rocket burns fuel at the rate $r(t) = 3000 - 50t$ kilograms per second for $0 \le t \le 60$ s. The total fuel consumed is

$$\int_0^{60} (3000 - 50t)\, dt = \left[3000t - 25t^2\right]_0^{60} = 180{,}000 - 90{,}000 = 90{,}000 \text{ kg}.$$

No physics beyond reading a rate and integrating it. The same calculation structure — $\int_a^b \text{(rate)} \, dt = \text{total change}$ — appears in pharmacokinetics (drug concentration over time), economics (consumer surplus under a demand curve), and electrical engineering (charge delivered by a current).

Where this leads. Mastering one-variable integration is the prerequisite for every dimension you add next. In multivariable calculus, the single integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ generalizes to double and triple integrals over regions in the plane and in space:

$$\iint_R f(x, y)\, dA, \qquad \iiint_V f(x, y, z)\, dV.$$

The same core idea — slice, approximate, sum, take the limit — still drives every calculation. Finding the mass of a non-uniform solid, computing the gravitational field of an irregular body, or evaluating a probability over a two-dimensional distribution all reduce to setting up the right bounds and iterating the one-variable technique you already own.

If there is one thread worth pulling next, it is the double integral. Setting up the region of integration, choosing the order of integration, and switching to polar coordinates when the geometry demands it are exactly the skills where one-variable fluency pays compound interest.
