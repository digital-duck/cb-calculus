Concept Book: Integral Calculus

Payoff

Integral calculus is the mathematical science of accumulation. Where differential calculus asks how fast a quantity changes at a point, integral calculus asks how much accumulates over an interval. Formally, for a function $f$ continuous on $[a, b]$, the definite integral

$$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k^*)\, \Delta x$$

encodes the limit of Riemann sums, collapsing infinitely many infinitesimal contributions into a single exact value. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus then reveals that differentiation and integration are inverse operations: if $F' = f$, then $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$. This duality makes integral calculus not merely a technique but a structural keystone of analysis.

Worked example. A particle moves along a line with velocity $v(t) = 3t^2 - 6t + 2$ (m/s). Its net displacement over $[0, 3]$ is

$$\int_0^3 (3t^2 - 6t + 2)\,dt = \bigl[t^3 - 3t^2 + 2t\bigr]_0^3 = (27 - 27 + 6) - 0 = 6 \text{ m}.$$

The integral converts a continuously varying rate into a total — a paradigm that recurs throughout mathematics and science.

Key theorem (Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part II). Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and $F$ any antiderivative of $f$. Then $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$. The proof follows from telescoping the Riemann sum over a partition and applying the Mean Value Theorem to each subinterval, showing the limit equals the net change in $F$.

Connection to multivariable calculus. The single-variable integral is the foundation upon which multivariable calculus is built. Double and triple integrals — $\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA$ and $\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV$ — extend accumulation to regions in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$, enabling the computation of volumes, masses, and flux. Line integrals generalize further, integrating along curves in space, and surface integrals over two-dimensional manifolds. The grand theorems of vector calculus — Green's, Stokes's, and the Divergence Theorem — each express a profound generalization of the Fundamental Theorem, relating an integral over a region to one over its boundary.

Lab cell.

from sympy import symbols, integrate, simplify

t = symbols('t')
v = 3*t**2 - 6*t + 2
displacement = integrate(v, (t, 0, 3))
print("Net displacement:", displacement)   # 6

Integral calculus is complete as a theory of one-dimensional accumulation, yet its power expands dramatically the moment the domain does. The reader who has followed this journey from Riemann sums to the Fundamental Theorem is now equipped to enter multivariable calculus — where the same ideas, lifted into higher dimensions, describe fluid flow, gravitational fields, and the geometry of curved surfaces. The invitation stands: take what you know about $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ and ask what happens when $f$ depends on two variables, and the domain $[a,b]$ becomes a region in the plane.
