Difference Quotient

Definition. The difference quotient of a function $f$ at $x$ with step size $h$ is

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}, \quad h \neq 0.$$

Geometrically, this is the slope of the secant line — the straight line connecting the two points $(x,\, f(x))$ and $(x+h,\, f(x+h))$ on the graph of $f$. It answers the question: how fast is $f$ changing, on average, over the interval from $x$ to $x+h$? As $h$ shrinks toward zero, the secant approaches the tangent line at $x$, and the difference quotient approaches the derivative $f'(x)$. Mastering this expression is the essential prerequisite for understanding all of differential calculus.

Worked example. Let $f(x) = x^2$. Compute the difference quotient and simplify.

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h}$$

Expand the numerator:

$$(x+h)^2 - x^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2 = 2xh + h^2$$

Divide by $h$ (valid since $h\neq 0$):

$$\frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h$$

So the difference quotient for $f(x)=x^2$ simplifies to $2x+h$. As $h \to 0$, this approaches $2x$ — which is exactly the derivative $f'(x)=2x$ you would get from the power rule.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you need to estimate the instantaneous speed of a falling object whose height (in meters) is modeled by $h(t) = -4.9t^2 + 20$ at $t=1$ second. You don't yet know calculus rules, but you can evaluate the difference quotient with a small step, say $\Delta t = 0.01$:

$$\frac{h(1.01)-h(1)}{0.01} = \frac{(-4.9(1.01)^2+20)-(-4.9(1)^2+20)}{0.01}$$

Compute: $h(1.01) = -4.9(1.0201)+20 \approx 14.9015$, $h(1)=15.1$, so

$$\frac{14.9015 - 15.1}{0.01} = \frac{-0.1985}{0.01} \approx -9.85 \text{ m/s.}$$

The negative sign confirms the object is falling. The exact answer $-4.9(2)(1) = -9.8$ m/s emerges in the limit — showing that the difference quotient is a computable, numerical bridge between average rates of change and instantaneous ones. Whenever you see a derivative, remember it is just a difference quotient with $h$ taken to its limit.
