Velocity

Velocity describes how quickly an object's position changes, and in which direction. This makes it a vector quantity — it carries both magnitude and direction — which distinguishes it from speed, a scalar that only tells you how fast. A car traveling 60 mph north and one traveling 60 mph south have the same speed but opposite velocities.

Average velocity over a time interval $[t_1, t_2]$ is the displacement divided by elapsed time:

$$\bar{v} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{x(t_2) - x(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1}$$

Note that displacement $\Delta x$ is the straight-line change in position, not total distance traveled. A runner who goes 400 m around a track and returns to the start has zero average velocity — but nonzero average speed.

Instantaneous velocity is the limit of that ratio as the interval shrinks to zero, which is the derivative of position with respect to time:

$$v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt}$$

Worked Example

A particle moves along a line with position $x(t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 5$ (meters, $t$ in seconds).

Find the velocity at $t = 3$ s:

$$v(t) = \frac{d}{dt}(3t^2 - 12t + 5) = 6t - 12$$ $$v(3) = 6(3) - 12 = 6 \text{ m/s}$$

The positive sign confirms the particle is moving in the positive direction at that instant. Setting $v(t) = 0$ gives $t = 2$ s — the moment the particle momentarily stops before reversing direction, the turning point.

Problem-Solving Application

Suppose you need to find when an object moving under constant acceleration $a$ first reaches a target velocity $v_f$, given it starts at $v_0$. Use the kinematic relation:

$$v(t) = v_0 + at \implies t = \frac{v_f - v_0}{a}$$

If a car accelerates from rest ($v_0 = 0$) at $a = 4 \text{ m/s}^2$ and you need to know when it hits 28 m/s (roughly 100 km/h):

$$t = \frac{28 - 0}{4} = 7 \text{ s}$$

The key problem-solving habit: always check the sign of your velocity answer. A negative result means motion in the negative (often leftward or downward) direction — not an error, but information.
