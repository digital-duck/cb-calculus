Instantaneous Rate Of Change

The average rate of change of a function $f$ over an interval $[a, b]$ is the familiar slope of a secant line: $\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$. But what if you want the rate of change right now — at a single instant — not across an interval? That is the instantaneous rate of change, and it is the central idea of differential calculus.

Formally, the instantaneous rate of change of $f$ at $x = a$ is defined as the limit of the average rate of change as the interval shrinks to zero:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$$

Geometrically, this is the slope of the tangent line to the curve at the point $(a,\ f(a))$. The secant line pivots around that point as $h \to 0$, converging on the tangent.

Worked example. A ball's height (in meters) at time $t$ seconds is $f(t) = -5t^2 + 20t$. How fast is it rising at exactly $t = 1\,\text{s}$?

$$f'(1) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(1+h) - f(1)}{h}$$

Expand the numerator:

$$f(1+h) = -5(1+h)^2 + 20(1+h) = -5(1 + 2h + h^2) + 20 + 20h = 15 + 10h - 5h^2$$ $$f(1) = -5 + 20 = 15$$

So:

$$f'(1) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(15 + 10h - 5h^2) - 15}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{10h - 5h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(10 - 5h) = 10$$

At $t = 1\,\text{s}$, the ball is rising at $10\,\text{m/s}$.

Problem-solving application. Once you recognize that an instantaneous rate of change is always a derivative, the strategy is consistent: (1) write out the limit definition with the specific function, (2) expand and simplify so that $h$ cancels in the denominator, (3) evaluate at $h = 0$.

Watch for this pattern in any applied field. Velocity is the derivative of position; marginal cost in economics is the derivative of total cost; the slope of a dose-response curve at a given dose is a derivative. Whenever a problem asks "how fast is X changing at this moment?", translate it directly into the limit above — then decide whether to evaluate it from first principles or by applying differentiation rules to get the answer more efficiently.
