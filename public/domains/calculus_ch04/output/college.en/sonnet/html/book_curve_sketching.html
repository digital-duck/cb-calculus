Concept Book: Curve Sketching

Calculus Ch04 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Curve sketching is the moment calculus stops being a collection of separate techniques and becomes a single, unified lens. Up to this point you have learned to compute derivatives, classify critical points, identify concavity, and locate asymptotes. Curve sketching is what happens when you use all of those tools at once, in sequence, to produce a complete and accurate picture of how a function behaves — without plotting hundreds of points.

The procedure is systematic. Given a function $f$, you determine its domain, intercepts, and end behavior first. Then you find $f'(x)$ to locate critical numbers and decide where the function is increasing or decreasing. Next, $f''(x)$ reveals concavity and inflection points. Finally, you assemble these facts into a coherent graph that could not be obtained by guess-and-check alone.

Worked example. Sketch $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$.

  • Domain and intercepts: $f$ is defined on $\mathbb{R}$; zeros at $x = 0, \pm\sqrt{3}$.
  • First derivative: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1)$. Critical points at $x = \pm 1$. $f$ is decreasing on $(-1, 1)$ and increasing outside that interval.
  • Second derivative: $f''(x) = 6x$. Concave down for $x < 0$, concave up for $x > 0$; inflection point at $(0, 0)$.
  • Values at critical points: $f(-1) = 2$ (local max), $f(1) = -2$ (local min).

    • Armed with these six facts you can draw the curve confidently — a cubic that rises from $-\infty$, peaks at $(-1, 2)$, falls through an inflection at the origin, reaches a trough at $(1, -2)$, then rises again to $+\infty$.

    Why this is the natural endpoint. Every technique you practiced — limits, derivative rules, the first- and second-derivative tests — was building toward this synthesis. Curve sketching forces you to read a function rather than just evaluate it. That skill transfers directly to optimization (locating the global best), to physics (reading position, velocity, and acceleration from a single graph), and to any quantitative field where understanding how a quantity changes matters as much as knowing its value.

    Pick one application that interests you most — whether it is designing a roller coaster profile, minimizing material cost in an engineering problem, or analyzing a revenue curve in economics — and trace every step of a curve sketch through it. The tools are the same; the insight is entirely new.
