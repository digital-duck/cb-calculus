Concept Book: Growth Rates

Payoff

Throughout this text we have built a ladder of ideas — limits, continuity, derivatives, and series — each rung providing the language needed to climb to the next. Growth rates is the capstone rung: the concept that lets us compare not merely the values of functions, but the speed at which those values change, and to do so in a way that is independent of constants and lower-order noise.

Definition. Let $f, g : \mathbb{R}_{>0} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ be eventually positive functions. We say $f$ grows no faster than $g$, written $f = O(g)$, if there exist constants $C > 0$ and $x_0$ such that $f(x) \le C\,g(x)$ for all $x \ge x_0$. When $f = O(g)$ and $g = O(f)$ simultaneously, the functions are asymptotically equivalent, written $f \asymp g$. The strict ordering $f = o(g)$ — read "$f$ is little-oh of $g$" — holds when $\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x)/g(x) = 0$, expressing that $g$ eventually dominates $f$ without any constant rescue.

Worked example. Compare $f(x) = 3x^2 + 100x$ and $g(x) = x^2$.

$$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2 + 100x}{x^2} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\left(3 + \frac{100}{x}\right) = 3.$$

Because the limit is a finite nonzero constant, $f \asymp g$. The coefficient $100$ and the linear term vanish at scale; only the degree of growth survives.

Key theorem (Growth-rate hierarchy). The following strict chain holds as $x \to \infty$:

$$1 \;=\; o(\log x) \;=\; o(x^\alpha) \;=\; o(x^\beta) \;=\; o(a^x) \;=\; o(x!)$$

for any $0 < \alpha < \beta$ and $a > 1$. Every class in this hierarchy is separated by the little-oh relation, meaning no constant multiplier can bridge the gap between successive classes.

Lab cell.

from sympy import symbols, log, factorial, oo, limit

x = symbols('x', positive=True)
pairs = [(log(x), x**0.5), (x**0.5, x), (x, 2**x), (2**x, factorial(x))]
for f, g in pairs:
    print(f"lim {f}/{g} =", limit(f/g, x, oo))

Running this cell confirms each ratio tends to zero, giving a computational proof of the hierarchy for these representative pairs.

Growth rates are the natural endpoint of this book because every quantitative discipline eventually asks the same question: which effect dominates in the long run? In algorithm analysis, the hierarchy dictates whether a program scales to large inputs — an $O(n \log n)$ sort is categorically preferable to an $O(n^2)$ one regardless of hardware. In population dynamics and epidemiology, the distinction between polynomial and exponential growth is the difference between a manageable trend and an outbreak that overwhelms any linear response. In financial mathematics, compound interest and continuously reinvested returns are exponential processes, and understanding their growth class is what justifies the time-value of money at the heart of every valuation model. In information theory, entropy rates and channel capacities are expressed as logarithmic quantities precisely because logarithms are the inverse of the exponential class, balancing the scale of large combinatorial counts.

Each of these domains uses the same underlying machinery developed here: a precise notion of comparison that strips away constants and focuses on qualitative behavior at scale. The tools are identical; only the interpretation changes.

You now stand at the threshold of any one of these fields. Choose the application that calls to you — perhaps the elegance of sorting algorithms, the urgency of epidemic modeling, or the rigor of information theory — and carry the growth-rate hierarchy with you as your first and most reliable compass.
