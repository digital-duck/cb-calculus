Concept Book: Locating Absolute Extrema

Calculus Ch04 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The machinery developed throughout this chapter — continuity arguments, the Extreme Value Theorem, critical-point classification via first and second derivatives — converges on a single procedural achievement: locating absolute extrema. Given a continuous function $f$ on a closed, bounded interval $[a, b]$, the method guarantees that the global maximum and global minimum values are found in a finite, exhaustive search. Evaluate $f$ at every critical point $c \in (a, b)$ satisfying $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ undefined, then compare these values against the endpoint values $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. The largest output is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum. No candidate can be overlooked, and no spurious candidates appear.

Worked example. Let $f(x) = 2x^3 - 9x^2 + 12x - 4$ on $[0, 3]$. Setting $f'(x) = 6x^2 - 18x + 12 = 6(x-1)(x-2) = 0$ yields critical points $x = 1$ and $x = 2$. Evaluating at all candidates:

$$f(0) = -4, \quad f(1) = 1, \quad f(2) = 0, \quad f(3) = 5.$$

The absolute maximum is $f(3) = 5$ and the absolute minimum is $f(0) = -4$. The local maximum at $x = 1$ is not the global winner — a fact invisible without comparing endpoints.

Theorem (Closed-Interval Method). If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, then

$$\max_{x \in [a,b]} f(x) = \max\!\bigl\{f(c_1), \ldots, f(c_k), f(a), f(b)\bigr\},$$

where $c_1, \ldots, c_k$ are all critical points of $f$ in $(a, b)$. The analogous statement holds for the minimum.

from sympy import *
x = symbols('x')
f = 2*x**3 - 9*x**2 + 12*x - 4
crits = solve(diff(f, x), x)
candidates = crits + [0, 3]
vals = {c: f.subs(x, c) for c in candidates}
print("Values:", vals)
print("Absolute max:", max(vals, key=vals.get), "→", max(vals.values()))
print("Absolute min:", min(vals, key=vals.get), "→", min(vals.values()))

Why this is the natural endpoint. Every optimization problem in the physical and social sciences ultimately asks: over all feasible states, which is best? The Closed-Interval Method is the rigorous answer when "feasible" means a bounded domain. It is the bridge between pure analysis and applied optimization: minimizing material cost in structural design, maximizing yield in a chemical reaction, finding the production level that peaks profit, or identifying the antenna angle that concentrates signal strength. In each case, the domain constraints set the interval, the physical law supplies the objective function, and the Closed-Interval Method delivers a provably correct answer.
