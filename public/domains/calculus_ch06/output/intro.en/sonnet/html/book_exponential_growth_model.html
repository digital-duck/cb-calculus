Concept Book: Exponential Growth Model

Calculus Ch06 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The exponential growth model is the inevitable destination of any rigorous treatment of proportional change. Recall the defining differential equation: a quantity $N(t)$ satisfies

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = r\,N(t), \qquad N(0) = N_0,$$

whose unique solution is $N(t) = N_0\,e^{rt}$. What makes this the natural endpoint of the preceding chapters is precisely the self-referential character of the equation — the rate of change is proportional to the current state. Every concept introduced so far (limits, derivatives, the fundamental theorem of calculus, separation of variables, initial-value problems) is not merely used here; each is required. Remove any one piece and the derivation collapses. The exponential growth model is, in this sense, the first theorem whose proof demands the full machinery of introductory calculus.

The model's reach across disciplines is remarkable. In population biology, $r$ encodes per-capita birth and death rates; doubling time $T_2 = \ln 2\,/\,r$ is directly measurable in the field. In finance, continuous compounding sets $N_0 = P$ (principal) and yields the familiar formula $A = P\,e^{rt}$, from which effective annual rates and present-value discounting both follow by algebra alone. In nuclear physics, the same equation governs radioactive decay with $r < 0$; the half-life $t_{1/2} = -\ln 2\,/\,r$ is the quantity reported on every radioisotope data sheet. In epidemiology, the early, unconstrained phase of an outbreak satisfies $\dot{I} \approx \beta I$ when the susceptible pool is nearly exhausted, making $e^{\beta t}$ the baseline against which control interventions are measured.

from sympy import symbols, exp, solve, ln, simplify

t, r, N0 = symbols('t r N_0', positive=True)
N = N0 * exp(r * t)

# Doubling time: solve N(t) = 2*N0
T2 = solve(N - 2*N0, t)[0]
print("Doubling time T₂ =", simplify(T2))   # ln(2)/r

# Half-life for decay (r → -r)
T_half = solve(N0*exp(-r*t) - N0/2, t)[0]
print("Half-life t½ =", simplify(T_half))   # ln(2)/r

With this single equation you can estimate tomorrow's bacterial colony, price a bond, date a fossil, and forecast the first week of a viral outbreak. Choose the domain that matters most to you — population, finance, physics, or epidemiology — and the next chapter will carry the model as far as the data can take it.
