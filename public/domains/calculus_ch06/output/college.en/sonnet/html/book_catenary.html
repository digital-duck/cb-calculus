Concept Book: Catenary

Payoff

Throughout this chapter we have built machinery—hyperbolic functions, variational calculus, and the Euler–Lagrange equation—each piece justified by the next. The catenary is the concept that makes every prior step feel inevitable. A uniform, inextensible chain of length $L$ suspended between two fixed points assumes the shape

$$y(x) = a \cosh\!\left(\frac{x}{a}\right), \qquad a = \frac{T_0}{w},$$

where $T_0$ is the horizontal tension at the lowest point and $w$ is the weight per unit length. This curve is not a parabola—a common misconception—but the unique minimizer of gravitational potential energy subject to the constraint of fixed arc length. It is, in a precise sense, what equilibrium looks like when a one-dimensional object hangs freely under its own weight.

Why the catenary is the natural endpoint. The derivation requires every tool introduced earlier. Hyperbolic functions appear not by convenience but by necessity: the differential equation governing the hanging chain, $y'' = \frac{w}{T_0}\sqrt{1 + (y')^2}$, has $\cosh$ as its unique solution satisfying the boundary conditions. The arc-length functional $\int \sqrt{1+(y')^2}\,dx$ and the Euler–Lagrange framework are the only machinery powerful enough to make this derivation rigorous. Arriving at $\cosh$ here closes the loop opened at the chapter's start.

Connection to classical architecture. An inverted catenary is a catenary arch. When every stone in such an arch carries only compressive stress—no bending moment—the structure is mechanically optimal. Robert Hooke stated this in 1675 as an anagram: "as hangs the flexible line, so but inverted will stand the rigid arch." The Gateway Arch in St. Louis and the dome of St. Paul's Cathedral both approximate this ideal. The mathematics is identical to the hanging-chain problem with the sign of gravity reversed.

A worked comparison. Consider a chain of length $L = 5$ m suspended between points $(\pm 2,\, 0)$ m. The parameter $a$ is determined by solving $2a\sinh(2/a) = 5$ numerically, giving $a \approx 1.317$ m. The sag at the midpoint is $y(0) - y(2) = a(\cosh(2/a) - 1) \approx 0.938$ m. A parabola fitted to the same endpoints predicts a different sag and accumulates error as span-to-sag ratio decreases—precisely the regime where bridge cables and power lines operate.

Key theorem (Uniqueness of the catenary). Among all smooth curves connecting two fixed points with a prescribed arc length $L$, the catenary is the unique critical point of the potential energy functional $U[y] = \int y\,\sqrt{1+(y')^2}\,dx$ under the arc-length constraint. Existence follows from the direct method in the calculus of variations; uniqueness follows from strict convexity of the integrand.

from sympy import *

x, a = symbols('x a', positive=True)
y = a * cosh(x / a)

arc_length_integrand = sqrt(1 + diff(y, x)**2)
arc_length_integrand_simplified = simplify(arc_length_integrand)
print("Arc-length element:", arc_length_integrand_simplified)   # cosh(x/a)

tension_eq = Eq(diff(y, x, 2), sqrt(1 + diff(y, x)**2) / a)
print("ODE satisfied:", simplify(tension_eq.lhs - tension_eq.rhs) == 0)

The output confirms that $ds = \cosh(x/a)\,dx$, so arc length along the catenary is itself a hyperbolic function—a satisfying internal coherence. The tools are in your hands.
