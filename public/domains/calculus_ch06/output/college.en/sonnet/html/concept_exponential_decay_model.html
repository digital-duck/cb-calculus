Exponential Decay Model

Definition. Many real-world quantities decrease at a rate proportional to their current size — the more you have, the faster you lose it. This produces exponential decay, described by:

$$Q(t) = Q_0 \, e^{-kt}$$

where $Q_0$ is the initial quantity, $k > 0$ is the decay constant (units: 1/time), and $t$ is elapsed time. The underlying differential equation is $\frac{dQ}{dt} = -kQ$, which simply says "the rate of loss is proportional to what remains." The solution to that equation is the formula above — a fact worth knowing, even if you derive it later in calculus.

A convenient rearrangement defines the half-life $t_{1/2}$: the time at which $Q$ falls to half its starting value.

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k} \approx \frac{0.693}{k}$$

Half-life and decay constant are two descriptions of the same phenomenon; knowing one immediately gives the other.

Worked example. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years. A charcoal sample from an archaeological site retains 38% of its original $^{14}$C. How old is the sample?

First, find $k$:

$$k = \frac{\ln 2}{5730} \approx 1.21 \times 10^{-4} \text{ yr}^{-1}$$

Then solve $0.38 = e^{-kt}$ for $t$:

$$t = \frac{-\ln(0.38)}{k} = \frac{0.968}{1.21 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 8{,}000 \text{ years}$$

The sample is roughly 8,000 years old.

Problem-solving application. The same logic applies outside physics. A drug with a 4-hour half-life is administered at 200 mg. A second dose is given 6 hours later. To find the total drug concentration in the body immediately after the second dose:

$$C = 200\,e^{-k \cdot 6} + 200, \quad k = \frac{\ln 2}{4}$$ $$C = 200 \cdot e^{-1.04} + 200 \approx 200(0.354) + 200 \approx 271 \text{ mg}$$

This kind of stacking calculation — summing decayed residuals from prior doses — appears in pharmacokinetics, environmental contamination modeling, and capacitor discharge analysis. The key habit: identify $Q_0$ and $t_{1/2}$ (or $k$) from context, write the formula, then solve algebraically. Exponential decay is one of the most transferable quantitative tools across science and engineering.
