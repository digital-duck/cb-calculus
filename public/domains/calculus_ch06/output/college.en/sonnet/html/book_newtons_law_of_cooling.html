Concept Book: Newtons Law Of Cooling

Calculus Ch06 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Newton's Law of Cooling is the natural endpoint of this concept-book because it is the first ordinary differential equation most students encounter whose solution is not merely an exercise in algebra but a genuine predictive instrument. Every idea developed in earlier chapters — limits, derivatives, the exponential function, and the method of separation of variables — converges here into a single, self-contained physical law that can be written down in one line and applied, without modification, across dozens of scientific domains.

Definition. Let $T(t)$ denote the temperature of an object at time $t$, and let $T_{\infty}$ denote the constant ambient temperature of the surrounding medium. Newton's Law of Cooling states that the rate of change of $T$ is proportional to the temperature excess:

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = -k\bigl(T(t) - T_{\infty}\bigr), \qquad k > 0.$$

The negative sign encodes the physical fact that a hotter object loses heat; $k$ is the positive cooling constant determined by the geometry and thermal conductivity of the object.

Worked Example. A cup of coffee at $90\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ is placed in a room held at $20\,^{\circ}\text{C}$. After five minutes the coffee has cooled to $70\,^{\circ}\text{C}$. Find $T(t)$ and determine when the coffee reaches $40\,^{\circ}\text{C}$.

Separating variables and integrating:

$$T(t) = T_{\infty} + \bigl(T_0 - T_{\infty}\bigr)e^{-kt} = 20 + 70\,e^{-kt}.$$

Using $T(5) = 70$ to solve for $k$:

$$k = -\frac{1}{5}\ln\!\left(\frac{50}{70}\right) = \frac{1}{5}\ln\!\frac{7}{5} \approx 0.0673\;\text{min}^{-1}.$$

Setting $T = 40$:

$$t = \frac{\ln(70/20)}{k} = \frac{\ln 3.5}{k} \approx 18.6\;\text{min}.$$

Key Theorem (Uniqueness of the exponential solution). Under Lipschitz continuity of the right-hand side, the initial-value problem $dT/dt = -k(T - T_{\infty}),\ T(0) = T_0$ has a unique solution $T(t) = T_{\infty} + (T_0 - T_{\infty})e^{-kt}$ on $[0,\infty)$. This guarantee means the model makes a single, falsifiable prediction — a cornerstone of scientific modeling.

Lab Cell.

from sympy import *
t, k, T0, Tinf = symbols('t k T_0 T_inf', positive=True)
T = Function('T')
ode = Eq(T(t).diff(t), -k*(T(t) - Tinf))
sol = dsolve(ode, T(t), ics={T(0): T0})
display(sol)
# Substitute coffee values and plot
expr = sol.rhs.subs([(T0, 90), (Tinf, 20), (k, Rational(1,5)*ln(Rational(7,5)))])
p = plot(expr, (t, 0, 60), xlabel='t (min)', ylabel='T (°C)', show=False)
p.show()

The law's reach extends far beyond the kitchen. In forensic science it underlies post-mortem interval estimation; in food safety it governs required cooling schedules for cooked meats; in electrical engineering it models the thermal runaway risk of power transistors; and in climate science it approximates the relaxation of ocean surface temperatures after a storm. Each domain substitutes different values of $k$ and $T_{\infty}$, but the mathematics is identical.

Pick the application that interests you most — forensic time-of-death analysis is a particularly striking place to start — and carry the single formula you have just derived into a setting where it becomes a tool of real-world decision-making.
