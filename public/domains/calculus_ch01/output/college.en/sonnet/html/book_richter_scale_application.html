Concept Book: Richter Scale Application

Calculus Ch01 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Throughout this text, we have built logarithms from first principles: the inverse relationship to exponential growth, the change-of-base identity, the derivative $\frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}$, and the compressive power that collapses multiplicative structure into additive arithmetic. The Richter scale application is the natural capstone because it demands every one of these ideas simultaneously, and because it answers a question that is both scientifically urgent and intuitively gripping: how do we place numbers on phenomena whose energies span twelve orders of magnitude?

Definition. The local magnitude $M_L$ of an earthquake, as introduced by Charles Richter in 1935, is defined by

$$M_L = \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{A}{A_0}\right),$$

where $A$ is the maximum ground-displacement amplitude recorded by a Wood-Anderson seismograph at a standard distance, and $A_0$ is a reference amplitude corresponding to a magnitude-zero event. Because the formula is a base-10 logarithm of a ratio, each integer step on the scale represents a tenfold increase in amplitude—and, since radiated seismic energy $E$ scales approximately as

$$\log_{10} E \approx 1.5\, M_L + 11.8 \quad (\text{energy in ergs}),$$

each integer step corresponds to roughly a $10^{1.5} \approx 31.6$-fold increase in energy released.

Worked example. Suppose seismograph A records a magnitude-6.0 event and seismograph B records a magnitude-8.0 event. The amplitude ratio is

$$\frac{A_B}{A_A} = 10^{8.0 - 6.0} = 10^2 = 100,$$

so the larger earthquake displaces the ground one hundred times more. The energy ratio is

$$\frac{E_B}{E_A} = 10^{1.5 \times 2} = 10^3 = 1000.$$

A difference that looks modest on the scale—two integer units—conceals a thousandfold difference in destructive energy. This is the compressive miracle that logarithms perform.

Key theorem (logarithmic compression). If a quantity $Q$ spans a range $[Q_{\min}, Q_{\max}]$ with $Q_{\max}/Q_{\min} = R \gg 1$, then $\log_{10} Q$ spans a range of only $\log_{10} R$ units. For seismicity, $R \sim 10^{12}$, so twelve orders of magnitude compress to a twelve-unit interval—legible on a single axis.

Lab cell.

import sympy as sp

M = sp.Symbol('M', real=True)
A0 = 1  # reference amplitude

# Amplitude as a function of magnitude
A = lambda m: 10**m * A0

# Energy ratio between two magnitudes
def energy_ratio(m1, m2):
    return 10**(1.5 * (m2 - m1))

print("Amplitude ratio (M8 vs M6):", A(8) / A(6))
print("Energy ratio   (M8 vs M6):", energy_ratio(6, 8))

The scale is not merely a convenient index; it is a precise mathematical object grounded in the logarithm's defining property. To explore this further, consider deriving the empirical Gutenberg-Richter relation $\log_{10} N = a - b\,M$, which predicts the frequency of earthquakes of each magnitude worldwide—an invitation to see how a single logarithmic identity encodes the statistical heartbeat of a restless planet.
