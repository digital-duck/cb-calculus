Slope

Slope measures how steeply a line rises or falls as you move from left to right. More precisely, it is the ratio of the vertical change to the horizontal change between any two points on the line:

$$m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

A positive slope means the line climbs; a negative slope means it falls; a slope of zero is a horizontal line; and an undefined slope (division by zero) corresponds to a vertical line. Because slope is constant along any straight line, it does not matter which two points you pick — you will always get the same ratio.

Worked example. A runner's position data shows she is at mile marker 2 at $t = 10$ minutes and at mile marker 6 at $t = 30$ minutes. What is the slope of her position-vs-time graph, and what does it represent?

Using the formula:

$$m = \frac{6 - 2}{30 - 10} = \frac{4}{20} = 0.2 \text{ miles per minute}$$

The slope equals her speed — $0.2$ miles per minute, or $12$ mph. This illustrates that slope is not just a geometric property; it carries a real-world unit and meaning whenever the axes represent physical quantities.

Problem-solving application. Slope appears whenever you need to quantify a rate of change. Suppose a company's revenue was \$40{,}000 in January and \$55{,}000 in April (three months later). The slope of the revenue-vs-time line is:

$$m = \frac{55{,}000 - 40{,}000}{3 - 0} = \frac{15{,}000}{3} = \$5{,}000 \text{ per month}$$

You can now project forward: at this rate, revenue in July ($t = 6$) would be approximately $40{,}000 + 5{,}000 \times 6 = \$70{,}000$. This is linear extrapolation — valid as long as the underlying trend remains roughly constant.

A common error is reversing the subtraction order for one coordinate but not the other. Always subtract in the same direction: $(y_2 - y_1)$ over $(x_2 - x_1)$, with the same point labeled "2" in both numerator and denominator. Mixing orders flips the sign and gives a slope that is off by a factor of $-1$.
