Concept Book: Compound Interest Application

Calculus Ch01 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Compound interest is the process by which interest earned in each period is added to the principal, so that subsequent interest is computed on the enlarged base. Formally, if a principal $P$ is deposited at an annual nominal rate $r$ compounded $n$ times per year, the accumulated value after $t$ years is

$$A(t) = P\!\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}.$$

As $n \to \infty$ the discrete compounding converges to continuous compounding, yielding the exponential growth law

$$A(t) = P e^{rt},$$

which is the natural endpoint of the study of exponential functions: a single, closed-form expression that unifies growth, decay, and discounting across every time scale.

Worked example. Suppose $P = \$1{,}000$, $r = 0.06$, compounded monthly ($n = 12$) for $t = 10$ years.

$$A(10) = 1000\left(1 + \frac{0.06}{12}\right)^{120} = 1000\,(1.005)^{120} \approx \$1{,}819.40.$$

Under continuous compounding the same inputs give $A = 1000\,e^{0.6} \approx \$1{,}822.12$, a difference of less than \$3 — illustrating why engineers and economists routinely substitute the continuous model without loss of practical accuracy.

Theorem (Effective Annual Rate). For a nominal rate $r$ compounded $n$ times per year, the equivalent annual rate $r_{\text{eff}}$ satisfying $1 + r_{\text{eff}} = \bigl(1 + r/n\bigr)^n$ is strictly increasing in $n$ and approaches $e^r - 1$ as $n \to \infty$. This follows directly from the monotonicity of $(1 + x/n)^n$ and the definition of $e$.

The theorem makes compound interest the conceptual keystone of financial mathematics: every instrument — bonds, mortgages, annuities, option pricing via Black–Scholes — reduces, at its core, to present-value discounting with this formula.

Lab cell.

from sympy import symbols, Rational, exp, limit, oo, simplify

P, r, n, t = symbols('P r n t', positive=True)
discrete = P * (1 + r/n)**(n*t)
continuous = limit(discrete, n, oo)
print(simplify(continuous))   # P*exp(r*t)

Run this cell and verify that SymPy recovers $Pe^{rt}$ automatically. Then replace the symbolic $r$ with $0.06$ and $t$ with $10$ to reproduce the numerical example above.

The path from simple percentages to $Pe^{rt}$ is now complete. To see how deeply this idea reaches, consider tracing it into one of its most consequential applications: the mathematics of loan amortisation, where the same formula determines every monthly mortgage payment made by millions of households — and where a one-percentage-point change in $r$ can shift lifetime interest costs by tens of thousands of dollars.
