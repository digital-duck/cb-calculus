Concept Book: Price Demand Application

Payoff

Throughout this text, we have built a ladder of ideas: limits gave precision to the notion of "approaching," derivatives quantified instantaneous rates of change, and optimization located the extrema that govern rational decisions. The concept of the price–demand application is the natural capstone because it fuses every one of those tools into a single, economically meaningful question: how does a firm's revenue respond to the price it sets?

Definition. Let $q = D(p)$ denote the demand function, where $p > 0$ is the unit price and $q \geq 0$ is the quantity demanded. Revenue is then

$$R(p) = p \cdot D(p).$$

The price elasticity of demand at price $p$ is

$$\varepsilon(p) = \frac{p}{D(p)}\,D'(p),$$

and the marginal revenue with respect to price is $R'(p) = D(p) + p\,D'(p)$.

Worked Example. Suppose $D(p) = 200 - 4p$. Then $R(p) = 200p - 4p^2$. Setting $R'(p) = 200 - 8p = 0$ gives the revenue-maximizing price $p^ = 25$, yielding $R(25) = 2500$. The elasticity at $p^ = 25$ is $\varepsilon(25) = \frac{25}{100}(-4) = -1$, confirming the classical result that revenue is maximized precisely where demand is unit-elastic.

Key Theorem (Revenue–Elasticity Criterion). For a differentiable demand function $D(p) > 0$,

$$R'(p) = D(p)\bigl[1 + \varepsilon(p)\bigr].$$

Hence $R'(p) > 0$ when $|\varepsilon| < 1$ (inelastic demand), $R'(p) = 0$ when $|\varepsilon| = 1$ (unit elastic), and $R'(p) < 0$ when $|\varepsilon| > 1$ (elastic demand). Proof sketch: expand $R'(p) = D + pD'$, factor out $D$, and recognize $pD'/D = \varepsilon$.

Lab Cell.

from sympy import symbols, diff, simplify, solve, Rational

p = symbols('p', positive=True)
D = 200 - 4*p
R = p * D
R_prime = diff(R, p)
p_star = solve(R_prime, p)[0]
elasticity = (p / D) * diff(D, p)
print(f"p* = {p_star}, R(p*) = {R.subs(p, p_star)}")
print(f"ε(p*) = {simplify(elasticity.subs(p, p_star))}")

This single framework unlocks an entire ecosystem of applied reasoning. Supply-chain managers use $\varepsilon(p)$ to decide whether a price cut grows or shrinks revenue. Public-health economists apply it to model how a sugar tax alters consumption. Subscription-platform analysts estimate $D(p)$ from A/B tests and then solve the same optimization you ran above.

Each domain is, at its core, asking the same question you just answered. Choose one — pricing a medication, setting a streaming-service tier, or taxing a carbon-emitting good — and you will find that the machinery developed in this chapter carries you, with only minor adaptation, all the way to a policy recommendation grounded in mathematics.
