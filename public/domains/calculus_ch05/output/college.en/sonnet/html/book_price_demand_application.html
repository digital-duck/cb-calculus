Payoff

Every concept in this book has been building toward one practical question: how should a seller set a price? Price-demand application is the answer — the skill of using a demand model to make concrete, quantifiable decisions about pricing, revenue, and market reach.

A demand curve $Q = D(P)$ tells you how many units consumers will buy at each price. On its own, that is just a description. Price-demand application turns it into a decision tool by connecting it to revenue, $R(P) = P \cdot D(P)$, and asking: where is $R(P)$ maximized? Where does a price cut gain enough new buyers to offset the lower margin per unit? Where does a price increase collect more margin but lose too many customers to be worthwhile? The answer lives in the elasticity of demand,

$$\varepsilon(P) = \frac{dD}{dP} \cdot \frac{P}{D(P)},$$

which measures the percentage change in quantity for a one-percent change in price. When $|\varepsilon| > 1$ (elastic demand), cutting price grows revenue; when $|\varepsilon| < 1$ (inelastic demand), raising price does. Revenue is stationary exactly where $|\varepsilon| = 1$.

Consider a ride-share company that estimates $D(P) = 4000 - 200P$ rides per hour in a city. Revenue is $R(P) = P(4000 - 200P) = 4000P - 200P^2$. Setting $R'(P) = 4000 - 400P = 0$ gives $P^ = \$10$ and $Q^ = 2000$ rides — a clean, actionable result derived in two lines of calculus.

That same reasoning extends across every domain where buyers respond to price. A public health agency uses it to predict how a sugar-beverage tax shifts consumption. A SaaS startup uses it to choose between a $9/month plan that maximizes subscribers and a $29/month plan that maximizes revenue. A textbook publisher uses it to weigh open-access adoption against licensing income. An airline uses it — with demand segmented by booking window — to fill every seat at the highest price each segment will bear. In each case the machinery is identical: model demand, write revenue as a function of price, optimize.

The most fascinating of these extensions is price discrimination — charging different customers different prices for the same product based on their willingness to pay. It is the strategy behind student discounts, airline fare classes, and streaming tiers, and it shows how a single demand curve contains far more extractable value than any uniform price can reach. If you want to see how elasticity, calculus, and market design combine into one of the most consequential tools in applied economics, that is where to go next.
