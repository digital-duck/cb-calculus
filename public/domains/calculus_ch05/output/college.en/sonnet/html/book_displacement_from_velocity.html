What Displacement from Velocity Achieves

Throughout this chapter we have built, layer by layer, the machinery of the definite integral. The concept of displacement from velocity is the moment that machinery becomes indispensable rather than merely convenient. Given a velocity function $v(t)$ defined on a closed interval $[a, b]$, the net displacement of a moving body is

$$\Delta x = \int_a^b v(t)\, dt.$$

This formula is not an approximation. It is an exact consequence of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: because displacement is an antiderivative of velocity, the definite integral recovers it precisely. The result is remarkable because $v(t)$ need not be constant, monotone, or even positive — the integral accounts for forward and backward motion automatically, with signed area encoding direction. This is the natural endpoint of the chapter because it is the first place where a genuinely continuous process — motion — yields to exact quantitative analysis.

Worked Example

Suppose a particle moves along a line with velocity $v(t) = 3t^2 - 6t$ metres per second for $t \in [0, 3]$. The net displacement is

$$\Delta x = \int_0^3 (3t^2 - 6t)\, dt = \Bigl[t^3 - 3t^2\Bigr]_0^3 = (27 - 27) - 0 = 0 \text{ m}.$$

The particle returns to its starting point, yet it has genuinely moved: on $[0, 2]$ it drifts backward, and on $[2, 3]$ it surges forward by the same magnitude. The total distance travelled, by contrast, requires $\int_0^3 |v(t)|\, dt = 4\,\text{m}$, a distinction the signed integral makes precise.

Key Theorem

Theorem (Displacement–Antiderivative Correspondence). If $v$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $x(t)$ is any antiderivative of $v(t)$, then

$$\int_a^b v(t)\, dt = x(b) - x(a).$$

Proof sketch. Define $F(t) = \int_a^t v(s)\, ds$. By the First Fundamental Theorem, $F'(t) = v(t)$, so $F$ and $x$ differ by a constant $C$. Evaluating $F(b) - F(a) = [x(b) + C] - [x(a) + C]$ yields the result. $\square$

Lab Cell (SymPy)

from sympy import symbols, integrate, Rational

t = symbols('t')
v = 3*t**2 - 6*t

displacement = integrate(v, (t, 0, 3))
distance     = integrate(abs(v), (t, 0, 3))

print("Net displacement:", displacement)   # 0
print("Total distance  :", distance)        # 4

Run this cell, then modify the interval to $[0, 2]$ and observe how the sign of displacement changes. The gap between displacement and distance is precisely the integral of $|v(t)|$ over the subinterval where $v < 0$.

With this single formula, $\Delta x = \int_a^b v(t)\, dt$, an entire landscape of problems becomes tractable: from computing the recoil of a rocket stage given its thrust profile, to reconstructing a patient's respiratory volume from a spirometry trace, to animating a character's path in a physics engine from sampled frame velocities. Each domain replaces the same abstract $v(t)$ with a concrete measured or modelled signal, and the integral does the rest.
