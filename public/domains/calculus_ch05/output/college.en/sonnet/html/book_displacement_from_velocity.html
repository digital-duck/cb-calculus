Concept Book: Displacement From Velocity

Calculus Ch05 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every step in this chapter has been building toward a single realization: if you know how something is changing, you can recover how much it has changed. Displacement from velocity is that realization made precise. Given a velocity function $v(t)$, the net displacement over a time interval $[a, b]$ is

$$\Delta x = \int_a^b v(t)\, dt.$$

This is not a trick or a shortcut — it is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus doing exactly what it was designed to do: undoing differentiation. Velocity is the derivative of position, so integrating velocity hands position back. The area under a velocity-time graph is the displacement, and now you have the machinery to compute it exactly rather than estimate it from a graph.

Worked example. A cyclist accelerates from rest with velocity $v(t) = 3t^2 - 6t + 4$ m/s. How far does she travel in the first 3 seconds?

$$\Delta x = \int_0^3 (3t^2 - 6t + 4)\, dt = \Bigl[t^3 - 3t^2 + 4t\Bigr]_0^3 = (27 - 27 + 12) - 0 = 12 \text{ m}.$$

Note that $v(t)$ dips below zero near $t = 1$, meaning she briefly moves backward — the integral accounts for this automatically, giving net displacement rather than total distance traveled. If you wanted total distance, you would integrate $|v(t)|$, splitting the interval at every zero crossing.

Why this is the natural endpoint. Once you can turn a rate into an accumulation, an entire landscape of problems becomes tractable. Dead-reckoning navigation — used in aircraft, submarines, and autonomous vehicles — estimates position by integrating accelerometer data over time when GPS is unavailable. In fluid dynamics, flow rate integrated over time gives total volume delivered through a pipe. In pharmacokinetics, the area under a drug-concentration curve (itself a rate of clearance) determines the total dose absorbed. In economics, marginal cost integrated over a production range yields total cost. Every one of these problems has the same skeleton: a rate is known, a cumulative quantity is needed.

The cyclist problem is clean because $v(t)$ is a polynomial. Real applications are rarely so tidy — $v(t)$ may be measured data rather than a formula, which is where numerical integration (the trapezoid rule, Simpson's rule, Gaussian quadrature) takes over. That bridge from the exact integral to the computable approximation is the natural next depth to explore: pick any one application above and ask how engineers actually evaluate the integral when no closed form exists.
