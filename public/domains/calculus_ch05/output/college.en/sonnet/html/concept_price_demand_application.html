Price Demand Application

The law of demand states that as the price of a good rises, the quantity consumers are willing to buy falls — all else equal. A demand function makes this relationship precise and computable. For a linear model, we write:

$$Q = a - bP$$

where $Q$ is quantity demanded, $P$ is price, and $a$, $b$ are positive constants estimated from market data. The slope $-b$ tells you how many fewer units sell for each one-dollar price increase.

Worked example. A coffee shop finds that when it charges \$3 per cup it sells 200 cups per day, and at \$4 it sells 160. We can fit a linear demand function to these two points.

The slope is:

$$b = -\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} = -\frac{160 - 200}{4 - 3} = 40$$

Using the point $(P, Q) = (3, 200)$:

$$200 = a - 40(3) \implies a = 320$$

So the demand function is $Q = 320 - 40P$.

Now the shop can answer business questions directly. At \$5 per cup: $Q = 320 - 40(5) = 120$ cups per day. Daily revenue is $R = P \cdot Q = 5 \times 120 = \$600$. At the original \$3 price, revenue was $3 \times 200 = \$600$ — identical. This suggests the shop sits near the revenue-maximizing price, where a small increase or decrease in price leaves revenue roughly unchanged.

Problem-solving application. To find the price that maximizes revenue, substitute $Q = 320 - 40P$ into $R = P \cdot Q$:

$$R = P(320 - 40P) = 320P - 40P^2$$

Taking the derivative and setting it to zero:

$$\frac{dR}{dP} = 320 - 80P = 0 \implies P^* = \$4$$

At \$4, the shop sells 160 cups and earns $R = \$640$ per day — the true peak.

This framework applies immediately to pricing decisions in retail, subscription services, and public policy (e.g., estimating the revenue effect of a tax). The key habit is to always express revenue as a function of price alone by substituting the demand equation, then optimize. When demand data comes in a non-linear form — say, $Q = aP^{-b}$ (a power-law or constant-elasticity model) — the same substitution-and-optimize logic applies; only the algebra changes.
