Net Change Theorem

The Net Change Theorem is a direct consequence of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. It states that if $F$ is a differentiable function whose derivative $F'$ is integrable on $[a, b]$, then

$$\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a).$$

In plain terms: the integral of a rate of change equals the net change in the quantity itself. "Net" is the key word — the integral accumulates positive and negative contributions, so it measures the overall difference from start to finish, not the total distance traveled or total amount produced.

Worked Example. Suppose a car's velocity (in m/s) is given by $v(t) = 3t^2 - 6t$ over the interval $[0, 4]$. Because velocity is the derivative of position, the net displacement is

$$\int_0^4 (3t^2 - 6t)\,dt = \left[t^3 - 3t^2\right]_0^4 = (64 - 48) - (0) = 16 \text{ m}.$$

The car ends up 16 m ahead of where it started. Notice, however, that on $[0, 2]$ the velocity is negative (the car moves backward), and on $[2, 4]$ it is positive. The net change theorem absorbs both contributions automatically, giving the signed result.

Problem-Solving Application. A water tank drains at a rate $r(t) = -2t + 8$ liters per minute, where $t$ is time in minutes. How much water leaves the tank over the first 5 minutes?

The net change in volume is

$$\int_0^5 (-2t + 8)\,dt = \left[-t^2 + 8t\right]_0^5 = (-25 + 40) - 0 = 15 \text{ L}.$$

The tank loses 15 liters. If instead you wanted the total volume that flowed out (ignoring any inflow), you would split the integral at the zero of $r(t)$ — but the net change theorem gives you the signed result directly, which is exactly what you need when tracking inventory, position, or stored energy.

The practical power of this theorem is that it lets you skip the step of finding an antiderivative formula explicitly: whenever you can model a real-world quantity as a rate, a definite integral immediately answers "how much did it change?" This framing applies to population growth, drug concentration in the bloodstream, electrical charge accumulation, and any other context where you know the derivative and need the cumulative effect.
