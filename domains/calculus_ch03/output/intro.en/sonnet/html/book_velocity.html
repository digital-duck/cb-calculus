Concept Book: Velocity

Calculus Ch03 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Velocity is the first derivative of position with respect to time, formally defined for a smooth path $\mathbf{r}(t) : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ as

$$\mathbf{v}(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{r}(t + \Delta t) - \mathbf{r}(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt}.$$

The limit encodes something profound: instantaneous rate of change is not a ratio of finite quantities but a boundary value. Every concept developed in this book — limits, continuity, the difference quotient — was scaffolding erected precisely so this definition could stand without circularity. Velocity is the natural endpoint of that construction because it is the simplest quantity whose meaning genuinely requires the limit process; displacement and speed do not.

Worked example. A projectile launched from the origin with initial speed $v_0$ at angle $\theta$ follows $\mathbf{r}(t) = \bigl(v_0 t \cos\theta,\; v_0 t \sin\theta - \tfrac{1}{2}g t^2\bigr)$. Differentiating component-wise gives

$$\mathbf{v}(t) = \bigl(v_0 \cos\theta,\; v_0 \sin\theta - g t\bigr).$$

At $t = 0$ the horizontal component is constant and the vertical component equals $v_0 \sin\theta$, confirming the physical intuition that gravity acts only along the vertical axis.

Theorem (Velocity determines trajectory up to initial condition). If $\mathbf{v}(t)$ is continuous on $[t_0, T]$ and $\mathbf{r}(t_0) = \mathbf{r}_0$, then $\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + \int_{t_0}^{t} \mathbf{v}(s)\, ds$ is the unique trajectory consistent with those data.

This theorem is the bridge to acceleration. Once $\mathbf{v}(t)$ is in hand, differentiating again yields $\mathbf{a}(t) = d\mathbf{v}/dt$, the quantity Newton's second law equates to force per unit mass. The entire edifice of classical mechanics — orbital mechanics, vibration analysis, fluid dynamics — is built on that single additional differentiation.

from sympy import *
t, v0, theta, g = symbols('t v_0 theta g', positive=True)
r = Matrix([v0*t*cos(theta), v0*t*sin(theta) - g*t**2/2])
v = diff(r, t)
a = diff(v, t)
display(v, a)

Running this cell confirms that $\mathbf{a}(t) = (0, -g)$, a constant vector pointing downward — the signature of uniform gravitational acceleration.

You now hold the key to kinematics. Turn to Chapter 5 to see how Newton's second law $\mathbf{F} = m\,\mathbf{a}$ transforms the abstract machinery of differentiation into quantitative predictions about every moving object in the universe.
