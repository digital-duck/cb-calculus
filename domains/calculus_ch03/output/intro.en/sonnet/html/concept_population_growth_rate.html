Population Growth Rate

Definition. Let $N(t)$ denote the size of a population at time $t$. The population growth rate is the derivative $\dfrac{dN}{dt}$, measuring how rapidly the population changes per unit time. The per-capita growth rate is defined as

$$r(t) = \frac{1}{N(t)}\frac{dN}{dt},$$

representing the average contribution of each individual to population change. When $r$ is constant, we obtain the exponential growth model: $\dfrac{dN}{dt} = rN$, with solution $N(t) = N_0 e^{rt}$, where $N_0 = N(0)$ is the initial population.

Worked Example. A bacterial culture begins with $N_0 = 500$ cells and doubles every $T = 20$ minutes. Find $r$ and predict the population after one hour.

From the doubling condition, $N(T) = 2N_0$, so $N_0 e^{rT} = 2N_0$, giving

$$r = \frac{\ln 2}{T} = \frac{\ln 2}{20} \approx 0.03466 \text{ min}^{-1}.$$

After $t = 60$ minutes:

$$N(60) = 500\, e^{(0.03466)(60)} = 500\, e^{2.0794} \approx 500 \times 8 = 4{,}000 \text{ cells}.$$

Since 60 minutes represents exactly three doubling periods, the population triples from one doubling to the next, confirming $N(60) = 500 \cdot 2^3 = 4{,}000$.

Key Theorem (Malthusian Growth). If the per-capita growth rate $r$ is constant and positive, then $N(t) \to \infty$ as $t \to \infty$; if $r < 0$, then $N(t) \to 0$. The quantity $t_{1/2} = \ln 2 / |r|$ gives the halving time when $r < 0$ and the doubling time when $r > 0$.

Proof sketch. Separating variables in $dN/N = r\,dt$ and integrating both sides yields $\ln N = rt + C$, so $N(t) = e^C e^{rt} = N_0 e^{rt}$. Monotonicity and the limiting behavior follow directly from the sign of $r$. $\square$

Lab Cell.

from sympy import *
t, r, N0 = symbols('t r N0', positive=True)

N = N0 * exp(r * t)
r_val = log(2) / 20          # doubling every 20 min
N_val = N.subs([(r, r_val), (N0, 500)])

print("N(60) =", N_val.subs(t, 60).evalf())   # 4000.0
print("Doubling time =", solve(N_val - 2*500, t))

Running this cell confirms $N(60) = 4{,}000$ and recovers the doubling time of 20 minutes, grounding the algebraic derivation in a reproducible computation.
