Derivative Function

The derivative function extends the idea of a derivative at a single point into a function defined across an entire domain. If $f(x)$ is differentiable on an interval, then its derivative $f'(x)$ assigns to each $x$ the instantaneous rate of change of $f$ at that point. Formally:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

The result is itself a function — one that tells you the slope of $f$'s tangent line everywhere at once. Alternative notations $\frac{dy}{dx}$, $\dot{y}$, and $Df(x)$ all refer to the same object.

Worked Example. Find the derivative function of $f(x) = x^3 - 4x$.

Apply the limit definition — or, more efficiently, the power rule $\frac{d}{dx}[x^n] = nx^{n-1}$ and linearity of differentiation:

$$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 4$$

Interpreting this: at $x = 0$, the slope is $-4$ (falling steeply); at $x = 2$, the slope is $3(4) - 4 = 8$ (rising). The derivative function encodes the entire slope portrait of $f$ in closed form.

Problem-Solving Application. Derivative functions are the engine behind optimization, curve sketching, and related-rates problems.

Finding extrema. Because $f'(x)$ gives the slope everywhere, the function $f$ can only have a local maximum or minimum where $f'(x) = 0$ or $f'(x)$ is undefined (critical points). For $f(x) = x^3 - 4x$:

$$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 4 = 0 \implies x = \pm\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$$

Evaluating the sign of $f'$ on either side of each critical point reveals whether $f$ increases or decreases there — a sign change from $+$ to $-$ confirms a local max; $-$ to $+$ confirms a local min.

Reading the derivative function as its own signal. Where $f'(x) > 0$, $f$ is increasing; where $f'(x) < 0$, $f$ is decreasing; where $f'(x) = 0$, $f$ is momentarily flat. Differentiating $f'$ again yields $f''(x) = 6x$, which describes concavity — positive means concave up (bowl shape), negative means concave down.

The derivative function transforms isolated slope calculations into a systematic toolkit: once you have $f'(x)$ in hand, questions about rates, extrema, and shape all reduce to algebraic work on a single expression.
