Concept Book: Population Growth Rate

Calculus Ch03 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every concept you have built toward — rates of change, exponential functions, carrying capacity, differential equations — converges here. The population growth rate is not merely another formula; it is the quantitative language that living systems use to describe their own futures. Mastering it means you can read a graph of disease spread, predict a city's housing demand, evaluate a conservation plan, or model a financial portfolio using the same underlying reasoning.

The core expression is deceptively compact. For a population $P(t)$, the instantaneous growth rate is

$$r = \frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dt}$$

That single quantity $r$ — births minus deaths per individual per unit time — encodes whether a population is expanding, contracting, or holding steady. When $r > 0$ the population grows; when $r < 0$ it declines; when $r = 0$ it is at equilibrium. In the simplest model, $\frac{dP}{dt} = rP$, the solution is the exponential $P(t) = P_0 e^{rt}$, which you can verify by differentiation. In the logistic model, $r$ is modulated by the carrying capacity $K$:

$$\frac{dP}{dt} = rP\!\left(1 - \frac{P}{K}\right)$$

Here, as $P \to K$, the effective growth rate $r(1 - P/K)$ approaches zero, giving the characteristic S-shaped curve seen in real populations from bacteria to blue whales.

Worked example. A wildlife survey records 400 elk in 2020 and 520 elk in 2025. Assuming exponential growth, solve for $r$: set $520 = 400\,e^{5r}$, so $r = \frac{\ln(520/400)}{5} \approx 0.053$ per year. At this rate the population doubles in $t_{2} = \ln 2 / r \approx 13$ years — a concrete forecast a conservation agency can act on.

Problem-solving reach. The same calculation structure applies directly to epidemiology (estimating the basic reproduction number $R_0$ from early case counts), to finance (continuous compounding at rate $r$), to urban planning (projecting school enrollment), and to evolutionary biology (comparing fitness coefficients across genotypes). In each domain, the question "how fast is this quantity growing relative to itself?" maps onto the same differential equation skeleton.

You have now assembled the full toolkit. Pick the application that pulls hardest at your curiosity — epidemic modeling is a particularly rich entry point, where $r$ splits into transmission and recovery rates and the math directly informs public health policy — and follow it as far as it goes.
