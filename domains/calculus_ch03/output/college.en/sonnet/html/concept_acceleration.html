Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes with respect to time. Because velocity is itself a vector — it has both magnitude and direction — acceleration occurs whenever an object speeds up, slows down, or changes direction, even if its speed stays constant. The formal definition is:

$$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v_f - v_i}{t_f - t_i}$$

In SI units, acceleration is measured in meters per second squared ($\text{m/s}^2$). When acceleration and velocity point in the same direction, the object speeds up. When they point in opposite directions, the object slows down — a condition sometimes called deceleration, though it is simply negative acceleration along the chosen axis.

For problems where acceleration is constant, four kinematic equations connect displacement ($x$), initial velocity ($v_0$), final velocity ($v$), acceleration ($a$), and time ($t$):

$$v = v_0 + at \qquad x = v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 \qquad v^2 = v_0^2 + 2ax \qquad x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v)\,t$$

You only need three known quantities to solve for a fourth — choose the equation that contains your unknown and avoids the quantity you were not given.

Worked example. A car starts from rest and reaches $28\ \text{m/s}$ in $7\ \text{s}$. Find its acceleration and the distance it travels.

$$a = \frac{28 - 0}{7} = 4\ \text{m/s}^2$$ $$x = v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = 0 + \tfrac{1}{2}(4)(7^2) = 98\ \text{m}$$

Applying the concept. Suppose a cyclist is moving at $12\ \text{m/s}$ and brakes with a constant deceleration of $3\ \text{m/s}^2$. How far does she travel before stopping?

Here $v = 0$, $v_0 = 12\ \text{m/s}$, $a = -3\ \text{m/s}^2$, and you are not given $t$ — so reach for $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2ax$:

$$0 = (12)^2 + 2(-3)x \implies x = \frac{144}{6} = 24\ \text{m}$$

Notice the strategy: identify the unknown, identify what is missing from the problem (here, time), then select the equation that skips the missing variable. This pattern — inventory your knowns, pick the right equation, solve algebraically before substituting numbers — applies to virtually every constant-acceleration problem you will encounter.
