Newtons Law Of Cooling

When a hot cup of coffee sits on your desk, it doesn't cool at a constant rate — it cools quickly at first, then more and more slowly as its temperature approaches room temperature. Newton's Law of Cooling captures this behavior precisely: the rate at which an object's temperature changes is proportional to the difference between the object's current temperature and the surrounding ambient temperature.

Formally, if $T(t)$ is the object's temperature at time $t$ and $T_a$ is the ambient (surrounding) temperature, then:

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - T_a)$$

where $k > 0$ is a cooling constant that depends on the object's material and surface properties. The negative sign ensures the temperature decreases when $T > T_a$. Solving this differential equation with initial condition $T(0) = T_0$ gives:

$$T(t) = T_a + (T_0 - T_a)\,e^{-kt}$$

This is an exponential decay toward the ambient temperature — exactly the behavior you'd observe with that coffee.

Worked Example. A cup of coffee starts at $90\,°C$ in a room at $20\,°C$. After 10 minutes it has cooled to $70\,°C$. What will its temperature be after 30 minutes?

Step 1: Use the 10-minute data point to find $k$.

$$70 = 20 + (90 - 20)\,e^{-10k} \implies 50 = 70\,e^{-10k} \implies e^{-10k} = \frac{5}{7}$$ $$k = -\frac{1}{10}\ln\!\left(\frac{5}{7}\right) \approx 0.03365\,\text{min}^{-1}$$

Step 2: Plug in $t = 30$.

$$T(30) = 20 + 70\,e^{-3 \times 0.03365 \times 10} = 20 + 70\left(\frac{5}{7}\right)^3 \approx 20 + 70(0.3644) \approx 45.5\,°C$$

Applying it to new problems. Newton's Law of Cooling appears wherever exponential thermal decay matters: forensic scientists estimate time of death by measuring a body's temperature against room temperature; food safety engineers verify that refrigerated goods cross below $4\,°C$ within a required time window; engineers size heat sinks for electronics. In each case, the strategy is identical — collect two temperature readings at known times to pin down $k$, then use the formula to predict temperatures at any other time or to solve for the time when a target temperature is reached.
