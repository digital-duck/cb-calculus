Hyperbolic Functions

Hyperbolic functions are exponential counterparts to the circular trigonometric functions. The two foundational definitions are:

$$\sinh x = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2}, \qquad \cosh x = \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2}$$

The remaining four follow the same pattern as their trig analogs:

$$\tanh x = \frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x}, \qquad \operatorname{sech} x = \frac{1}{\cosh x}, \qquad \operatorname{csch} x = \frac{1}{\sinh x}, \qquad \coth x = \frac{\cosh x}{\sinh x}$$

The name "hyperbolic" reflects the parametric identity $\cosh^2 t - \sinh^2 t = 1$, which traces the unit hyperbola $x^2 - y^2 = 1$, just as $(\cos t, \sin t)$ traces the unit circle. The key algebraic identities mirror circular trig almost exactly — with one critical sign flip. For derivatives: $\frac{d}{dx}\sinh x = \cosh x$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\cosh x = \sinh x$ (positive, unlike the $-\sin x$ of circular trig). This sign difference is the source of most errors, so notice it once and remember it always.

Worked example. A hanging power cable forms a catenary with shape $y = a\cosh(x/a)$. Find the arc length from $x = -L$ to $x = L$.

Arc length requires $\int_{-L}^{L}\sqrt{1 + (y')^2}\,dx$. Since $y' = \sinh(x/a)$, the Pythagorean identity gives:

$$1 + \sinh^2(x/a) = \cosh^2(x/a)$$

so the integrand simplifies to $\cosh(x/a)$, and the arc length is $2a\sinh(L/a)$. The identity did the heavy lifting; no messy exponential algebra was needed.

Problem-solving application. Hyperbolic functions are the natural solution to differential equations of the form $y'' = k^2 y$ (positive right-hand side). These arise in heat transfer, structural mechanics, and electrical transmission lines — anywhere exponential growth and decay combine with boundary conditions at both ends.

Consider a cooling fin with base temperature $T_0$, length $L$, and an insulated tip ($T'(L)=0$). The temperature satisfies $T'' = m^2 T$, giving solution:

$$T(x) = T_0\frac{\cosh(m(L-x))}{\cosh(mL)}$$

The heat flux at the base is $q = kAT_0 m\tanh(mL)$. The factor $\tanh(mL) \in (0,1)$ is the fin efficiency — it tells you what fraction of an ideal fin's heat transfer you actually get. Long fins ($mL \gg 1$) saturate at $\tanh \approx 1$; short fins waste little material but transfer proportionally less heat. Recognizing the ODE structure immediately tells you the solution involves $\cosh$ and $\sinh$, letting you apply boundary conditions without guessing exponential forms separately.
