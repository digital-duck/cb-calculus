Concept Book: Exponential Decay Model

Calculus Ch06 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every concept in this book has been building toward a single, powerful idea: when a quantity changes at a rate proportional to how much of it remains, the result is always the same shape — an exponential curve.

The exponential decay model formalizes that shape. If a quantity $Q$ decreases at a rate proportional to its current value, then

$$Q(t) = Q_0\, e^{-kt}, \quad k > 0,$$

where $Q_0$ is the initial amount and $k$ is the decay constant governing how quickly $Q$ diminishes. This is not an approximation or a special-case trick — it is the exact solution to the differential equation $\frac{dQ}{dt} = -kQ$, which appears whenever a process is self-limiting in proportion to what remains. That is why the model recurs across physics, chemistry, medicine, finance, and forensic science: the underlying structure is the same even when the units and domain are completely different.

Worked example. A 200 mg dose of ibuprofen has a half-life of roughly 2 hours in the bloodstream. The half-life condition gives $Q_0 e^{-k \cdot 2} = \frac{Q_0}{2}$, so $k = \frac{\ln 2}{2} \approx 0.347\ \text{hr}^{-1}$. After 6 hours, $Q(6) = 200\, e^{-0.347 \cdot 6} \approx 25\ \text{mg}$ — one-eighth of the original dose, exactly as the half-life formula predicts.

Problem-solving application. The model's real power is inversion: given measurements at two times, you can recover both $Q_0$ and $k$ from a system of two equations, then project forward or backward in time. Forensic analysts use exactly this to estimate when a drug was administered; engineers use it to predict when a radioactive source will fall below a safe emission threshold. The unknown you care about — initial quantity, time elapsed, or rate constant — is always the one you solve for algebraically after fitting the model to data.

Of the applications this model unlocks, Newton's Law of Cooling is the most immediately testable in everyday life: pull a hot mug of coffee from the microwave and you have a live experiment. The cooling curve obeys $T(t) = T_{\text{amb}} + (T_0 - T_{\text{amb}})\,e^{-kt}$, a direct extension of exponential decay to a shifted baseline. Exploring Newton's Law will show you how to extract $k$ from two temperature readings, predict when your coffee hits a drinkable 60 °C, and understand why the first few minutes of cooling are always the fastest.
