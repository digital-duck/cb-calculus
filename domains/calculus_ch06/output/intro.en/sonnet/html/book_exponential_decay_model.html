What the Exponential Decay Model Achieves

Throughout this chapter we have built, layer by layer, the analytic machinery needed to describe quantities that diminish at a rate proportional to their current size. The exponential decay model is the precise statement of that structure. Given a quantity $Q(t)$ satisfying

$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = -\lambda Q, \qquad \lambda > 0,$$

the unique solution subject to the initial condition $Q(0) = Q_0$ is

$$Q(t) = Q_0\, e^{-\lambda t}.$$

This is not merely a formula; it is a characterization theorem. Any process whose instantaneous rate of change is negatively proportional to its present value must follow this curve — there is no other continuous, differentiable function with that property. The exponential decay model is therefore the natural endpoint of our development: it is the universal attractor for a broad class of physical, biological, and financial phenomena, and it is fully determined by two parameters, $Q_0$ and $\lambda$, which are directly measurable.

Connection to Newton's Law of Cooling

Newton's Law of Cooling furnishes the most tactile illustration of exponential decay. Let $T(t)$ denote the temperature of an object immersed in an ambient environment held at constant temperature $T_\infty$. Newton's empirical law asserts that heat loss is proportional to the temperature excess:

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = -k\bigl(T(t) - T_\infty\bigr), \qquad k > 0.$$

Setting $u(t) = T(t) - T_\infty$ transforms this into $\dot{u} = -ku$, which is precisely the canonical decay equation. Its solution,

$$T(t) = T_\infty + (T_0 - T_\infty)\,e^{-kt},$$

predicts that the gap between object and environment closes exponentially. The decay constant $k$ encapsulates the thermal conductivity and surface geometry of the object; it can be estimated from two temperature readings at known times via

$$k = \frac{1}{t_2 - t_1}\ln\!\frac{T_0 - T_\infty}{T(t_2) - T_\infty}.$$

Worked Example. A cup of coffee at $90^\circ\text{C}$ cools in a $20^\circ\text{C}$ room. After five minutes its temperature is $70^\circ\text{C}$. Estimate $k$ and predict the temperature at $t = 15$ min.

$$k = \frac{1}{5}\ln\frac{90-20}{70-20} = \frac{1}{5}\ln\frac{70}{50} \approx 0.0673\ \text{min}^{-1}.$$ $$T(15) = 20 + 70\,e^{-0.0673\times 15} \approx 20 + 70\times 0.364 \approx 45.5^\circ\text{C}.$$ 
import sympy as sp

t, k, T0, Tinf = sp.symbols('t k T_0 T_inf', positive=True)
T = Tinf + (T0 - Tinf) * sp.exp(-k * t)

k_val = sp.Rational(1, 5) * sp.log(sp.Rational(70, 50))
T_numeric = T.subs({T0: 90, Tinf: 20, k: k_val})
print(sp.simplify(T_numeric.subs(t, 15)))   # ≈ 45.5

Invitation

Newton's Law of Cooling is merely the opening door. The same differential equation governs radioactive decay, the discharge of a capacitor, and the fade of a drug concentration in the bloodstream. Choose any one of these domains, gather two data points, fit $\lambda$, and the entire future trajectory — and the concept of half-life, $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/\lambda$ — follows for free.
