Number E

The number $e \approx 2.71828\ldots$ is one of the most important constants in mathematics, rivaling $\pi$ in how often it appears across science and engineering. It is defined as the limit

$$e = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$$

This definition is not arbitrary. It arises naturally from compound interest: if you invest \$1 at 100% annual interest compounded $n$ times per year, your balance after one year is $\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$. As compounding becomes continuous ($n \to \infty$), the balance converges to exactly $e$ dollars — no more, no matter how finely you subdivide. This is why $e$ is the natural base for continuous growth and decay.

The function $f(x) = e^x$ has a unique property: it is its own derivative. That is, $\frac{d}{dx} e^x = e^x$. No other base has this property, which is why $e$ simplifies calculus dramatically — exponential models written in base $e$ differentiate and integrate without extra scale factors.

Worked example. A bacterial culture starts with 500 cells and grows continuously at a rate of 3% per hour. The population at time $t$ hours is $P(t) = 500\, e^{0.03t}$. How long until the culture doubles?

Set $P(t) = 1000$:

$$500\, e^{0.03t} = 1000 \implies e^{0.03t} = 2 \implies 0.03t = \ln 2 \implies t = \frac{\ln 2}{0.03} \approx 23.1 \text{ hours}$$

This is the doubling time formula: $t_{\text{double}} = \frac{\ln 2}{r}$, where $r$ is the continuous growth rate.

Problem-solving application. Radioactive carbon-14 decays with a continuous rate $r = -0.000121$ per year. A fossil sample has 40% of the original carbon-14 remaining. Find its age.

Use $A(t) = A_0\, e^{rt}$ and set $A(t)/A_0 = 0.40$:

$$e^{-0.000121\, t} = 0.40 \implies t = \frac{\ln(0.40)}{-0.000121} \approx \frac{-0.916}{-0.000121} \approx 7{,}570 \text{ years}$$

The key skill is isolating the exponent using the natural logarithm $\ln$, since $\ln$ and $e^x$ are inverse functions: $\ln(e^x) = x$. Whenever a quantity grows or decays continuously — populations, drug concentrations, charge on a capacitor — the model involves $e$, and solving for time or rate always reduces to a single application of $\ln$.
