Compound Interest Application

Compound interest is the mechanism by which interest is calculated not just on the original principal but also on all previously accumulated interest. This "interest on interest" effect causes balances to grow exponentially rather than linearly, and understanding it is essential for evaluating loans, savings accounts, retirement accounts, and investment returns.

The core formula is:

$$A = P\!\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}$$

where $P$ is the principal, $r$ is the annual interest rate (as a decimal), $n$ is the number of compounding periods per year, $t$ is time in years, and $A$ is the final amount. When compounding happens continuously, the formula collapses to $A = Pe^{rt}$.

Worked example. Suppose you invest \$5{,}000 at an annual rate of 6%, compounded monthly, for 10 years. Here $P = 5000$, $r = 0.06$, $n = 12$, $t = 10$.

$$A = 5000\!\left(1 + \frac{0.06}{12}\right)^{12 \times 10} = 5000\,(1.005)^{120}$$

Computing $(1.005)^{120} \approx 1.8194$, so $A \approx \$9{,}097$. You nearly doubled your money without adding another dollar — the extra \$4{,}097 came entirely from compound growth.

Problem-solving application. Compound interest problems typically come in three flavors: find the future value $A$ (shown above), find the required principal $P$ (present value), or find the time $t$ needed to reach a target.

Suppose you need \$20{,}000 in 8 years for a down payment. Your account earns 5% compounded quarterly. How much should you deposit today?

Rearrange the formula for $P$:

$$P = \frac{A}{\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}} = \frac{20000}{\left(1.0125\right)^{32}} \approx \frac{20000}{1.4881} \approx \$13{,}441$$

You would need to deposit about \$13{,}441 today. Notice that a higher compounding frequency ($n$) or a longer time horizon $t$ both amplify growth — so when comparing financial products, always check both the rate and the compounding schedule, not just the advertised annual rate. The effective annual rate (EAR), $\left(1 + r/n\right)^n - 1$, is the single number that lets you compare products on equal footing regardless of how frequently they compound.
