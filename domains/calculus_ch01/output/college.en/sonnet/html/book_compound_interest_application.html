Concept Book: Compound Interest Application

Payoff

Compound interest is the mechanism by which money makes money on its own prior earnings. The core formula,

$$A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt},$$

says: a principal $P$ invested at annual rate $r$, compounded $n$ times per year for $t$ years, grows to $A$. When compounding is continuous, this collapses to $A = Pe^{rt}$. What makes this the natural capstone of the course is not the formula itself — it is that everything built earlier (exponential functions, logarithms, sequences, limits, even basic probability) converges here into a single engine you can point at real financial decisions.

The worked example makes the stakes concrete. Suppose you invest \$5,000 at 7% annual return, compounded monthly, and want to know when it doubles. Set $A = 10{,}000$:

$$10000 = 5000\left(1 + \frac{0.07}{12}\right)^{12t}$$ $$2 = \left(1.00583\right)^{12t}$$ $$12t = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln 1.00583} \approx \frac{0.6931}{0.005817} \approx 119.1$$

So $t \approx 9.9$ years. The Rule of 72 gives the same answer in seconds: $72 / 7 \approx 10.3$ years — a fast sanity check worth memorizing.

The problem-solving reach of this one formula is broad. Savings and retirement planning reduce to choosing $P$, $r$, $n$, and $t$ to meet a target $A$ — or inverting for $P$ (present value) to know what a future sum is worth today. Loan amortization — mortgages, car loans, student debt — applies the same arithmetic in reverse: each payment chips away at a balance that is itself compounding. Credit card debt, where $r$ might be 24% compounded daily, shows the same exponential curve working against you. Inflation and purchasing power erode savings at a compounding rate, making real (inflation-adjusted) return the quantity that actually matters: $r_{\text{real}} \approx r_{\text{nominal}} - \pi$. Even population growth and radioactive decay follow the same $e^{rt}$ skeleton, connecting finance to biology and physics through a single underlying structure.

Pick one of these threads and follow it all the way down. Retirement planning — how much to save monthly to reach a target at 65 — is perhaps the one whose answer will matter most personally. The mathematics is not hard; the discipline of sitting down and calculating is.
