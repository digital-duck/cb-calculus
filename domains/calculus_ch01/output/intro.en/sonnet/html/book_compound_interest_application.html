What Compound Interest Achieves

Compound interest is the mathematical engine behind every process in which a quantity grows in proportion to its current size. Having built fluency with limits, exponential functions, and differential equations, we arrive at compound interest not as a curiosity of finance, but as the canonical instantiation of multiplicative growth — the point at which all preceding machinery earns its keep.

Formally, if a principal $P$ is invested at an annual nominal rate $r$, compounded $n$ times per year over $t$ years, the accumulated value is

$$A(t) = P\!\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}.$$

Taking the limit as $n \to \infty$ yields the continuous-compounding formula,

$$A(t) = Pe^{rt},$$

which is simultaneously the unique solution to the initial-value problem $\dfrac{dA}{dt} = rA$, $A(0) = P$. This equivalence — limit of a discrete sequence equals solution of a differential equation — is the payoff of the entire first unit of analysis.

Worked Example

Suppose $P = \$1{,}000$, $r = 0.06$, and $t = 10$ years. Under monthly compounding ($n = 12$),

$$A = 1000\!\left(1 + \frac{0.06}{12}\right)^{120} = 1000\,(1.005)^{120} \approx \$1{,}819.40.$$

Under continuous compounding,

$$A = 1000\,e^{0.6} \approx \$1{,}822.12.$$

The difference of roughly $\$2.72$ quantifies exactly how much of the intuitive "interest on interest" story is captured only at the infinite-compounding limit.

Key Theorem

Theorem (Equivalence of Continuous Compounding and Exponential Growth). For any $r \in \mathbb{R}$ and $t \geq 0$,

$$\lim_{n \to \infty}\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt} = e^{rt}.$$

Proof sketch. Write $\left(1+\tfrac{r}{n}\right)^{nt} = \exp\!\left(nt\ln\!\left(1+\tfrac{r}{n}\right)\right)$. A first-order Taylor expansion gives $\ln(1+u) \approx u$ for small $u$, so $nt \cdot \tfrac{r}{n} \to rt$, and continuity of $\exp$ completes the argument. $\square$

Lab Cell

from sympy import symbols, limit, exp, ln, oo, simplify

r, t, n = symbols('r t n', positive=True)
expr = (1 + r/n)**(n*t)
result = limit(expr, n, oo)
print(simplify(result))   # exp(r*t)

Running this cell closes the loop between algebraic manipulation and symbolic verification — the computer confirms what the theorem promises.

The reach of this result extends far beyond a savings account. Radioactive decay, population dynamics, pharmacokinetic drug clearance, and the Black-Scholes pricing of options all rest on the same differential equation $\dot{y} = ry$. Each domain reinterprets $P$, $r$, and $t$, yet the mathematics is identical. Choose one of these applications — say, the half-life model in nuclear medicine — and you will find that every technique introduced in this book applies directly, without modification. That is the true payoff: a single, precisely understood idea that speaks fluently across disciplines.
