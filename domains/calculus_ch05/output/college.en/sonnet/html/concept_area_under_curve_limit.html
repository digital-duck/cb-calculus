Area Under Curve Limit

The area between a curve $y = f(x)$ and the $x$-axis cannot be computed directly from geometry formulas — but it can be approximated by rectangles, and then made exact by taking a limit. This is the central idea behind the definite integral.

Divide the interval $[a, b]$ into $n$ equal subintervals, each of width $\Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n}$. On the $i$-th subinterval, choose any sample point $x_i^$ and build a rectangle of height $f(x_i^)$. The sum of all rectangle areas is a Riemann sum:

$$S_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x$$

As $n \to \infty$, the rectangles become infinitely thin and the approximation becomes exact. The definite integral is defined as this limit:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x$$

This limit exists (and equals a finite number) whenever $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$.

Worked example. Find the area under $f(x) = x^2$ on $[0, 1]$ using the limit definition. Using right endpoints, $x_i^* = \frac{i}{n}$:

$$S_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(\frac{i}{n}\right)^2 \cdot \frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{n^3}\sum_{i=1}^{n} i^2 = \frac{1}{n^3} \cdot \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$$

Simplifying:

$$S_n = \frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2} = \frac{2n^2 + 3n + 1}{6n^2}$$

Taking the limit:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} S_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{2n^2 + 3n + 1}{6n^2} = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$$

So $\int_0^1 x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{3}$, confirmed by the antiderivative $\frac{x^3}{3}\Big|_0^1$.

Problem-solving application. In practice, you rarely compute limits of Riemann sums by hand — the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus lets you evaluate definite integrals via antiderivatives. But understanding the limit definition matters when the integrand has no closed-form antiderivative (for example, $e^{-x^2}$), because numerical methods like the midpoint rule and Simpson's rule are just controlled approximations of the same Riemann sum structure. Knowing that the limit exists, and how fast it converges, tells you how many subintervals you need to achieve a target accuracy.
