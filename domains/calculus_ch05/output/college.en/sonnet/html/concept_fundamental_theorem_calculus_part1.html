Fundamental Theorem Calculus Part1

Definition

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1 (FTC1) is the result that formally unifies differentiation and integration — two operations that look completely different but turn out to be inverse processes.

Theorem (FTC1). Let $f$ be continuous on $[a, b]$, and define the accumulation function

$$F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\, dt, \quad x \in [a, b].$$

Then $F$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$, and

$$F'(x) = f(x).$$

In plain language: if you build a running total of the area under $f$ from a fixed left endpoint $a$ up to a moving right endpoint $x$, then the rate at which that area grows equals $f(x)$ — the height of the curve at $x$. The dummy variable $t$ is used inside the integral simply to avoid a clash with the upper limit $x$.

Worked Example

Let $f(t) = 3t^2$ and define $F(x) = \int_0^x 3t^2\, dt$.

You can evaluate the integral directly: $F(x) = \left[t^3\right]_0^x = x^3$.

Differentiating: $F'(x) = 3x^2 = f(x)$. ✓

Now try the same problem without computing the integral first. FTC1 tells you immediately that $F'(x) = f(x) = 3x^2$ — no antiderivative calculation needed. This is the practical payoff.

Problem-Solving Application

FTC1 is most useful when the upper limit is a function of $x$ rather than $x$ itself. In that case, chain rule combines with FTC1.

Example. Let $G(x) = \int_1^{x^3} \cos(t^2)\, dt$.

You cannot find a closed-form antiderivative for $\cos(t^2)$, so direct evaluation is impossible. But with the chain rule substitution $u = x^3$:

$$G'(x) = \cos\!\left((x^3)^2\right) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(x^3) = \cos(x^6)\cdot 3x^2.$$

The theorem converts an otherwise intractable problem into a two-step application of rules you already know.

What to watch for: FTC1 applies only when $f$ is continuous on the interval containing both the fixed endpoint and $x$. If $f$ has a discontinuity in that range, $F$ may fail to be differentiable at that point, and the theorem does not apply there.
