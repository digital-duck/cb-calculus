Integrals Exponential Functions

The exponential function $e^x$ has a remarkable property: it is its own derivative. That same property makes it its own antiderivative. For any constant $C$,

$$\int e^x \, dx = e^x + C.$$

For a base other than $e$, writing $a^x = e^{x \ln a}$ gives the rule

$$\int a^x \, dx = \frac{a^x}{\ln a} + C, \quad a > 0,\; a \neq 1.$$

Both rules extend immediately through $u$-substitution. If the exponent is a linear function $kx$, substituting $u = kx$ yields

$$\int e^{kx} \, dx = \frac{1}{k} e^{kx} + C.$$

Worked example. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int 3e^{-2x}\, dx$.

Let $u = -2x$, so $du = -2\,dx$, which means $dx = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,du$. Substituting:

$$\int 3e^{-2x}\,dx = 3 \int e^u \left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right) du = -\frac{3}{2} e^u + C = -\frac{3}{2}e^{-2x} + C.$$

Differentiate the answer to verify: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\!\left(-\tfrac{3}{2}e^{-2x}\right) = -\tfrac{3}{2} \cdot (-2)e^{-2x} = 3e^{-2x}$. ✓

Problem-solving application. Suppose a bacteria colony grows at a rate of $R(t) = 500 e^{0.4t}$ cells per hour, where $t$ is measured in hours. How many new cells are added between $t = 0$ and $t = 3$?

The total count added is the definite integral of the rate:

$$\int_0^3 500 e^{0.4t}\, dt = 500 \cdot \frac{1}{0.4} e^{0.4t}\Bigg|_0^3 = 1250\left(e^{1.2} - e^{0}\right).$$

Since $e^{1.2} \approx 3.320$,

$$1250(3.320 - 1) = 1250 \times 2.320 \approx 2900 \text{ cells.}$$

The pattern here applies broadly: exponential functions model any process where the rate of change is proportional to the current amount — population, drug concentration, radioactive decay, compound interest. In every case, the integral of the rate is an exponential evaluated at the bounds, scaled by $1/k$. Recognizing that structure is the core skill; the arithmetic follows mechanically.
