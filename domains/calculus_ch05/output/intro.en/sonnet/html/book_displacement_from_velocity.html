Concept Book: Displacement From Velocity

Calculus Ch05 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The Central Achievement

Throughout this text we have built, layer by layer, the machinery of the integral. The concept of displacement from velocity is where that machinery earns its keep. Given a velocity function $v(t)$ defined on an interval $[a, b]$, the displacement of a moving body is

$$\Delta x = \int_a^b v(t)\, dt.$$

This single expression is the natural endpoint of the development because it unifies two ideas that initially appear unrelated: the antiderivative (an algebraic object) and the signed area under a curve (a geometric object). The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus guarantees they are the same thing, and displacement is the clearest physical witness to that identity. Velocity is the derivative of position; recovering position from velocity by integration is therefore not a trick but a logical inevitability.

Worked Example

Let $v(t) = 3t^2 - 6t$ on $[0, 3]$ (metres per second, $t$ in seconds). The displacement is

$$\Delta x = \int_0^3 (3t^2 - 6t)\, dt = \left[t^3 - 3t^2\right]_0^3 = (27 - 27) - 0 = 0\ \text{m}.$$

The body returns to its starting point, even though it was never stationary on $(0, 2)$: it travelled forward on $[0, 2]$ and backward on $[2, 3]$, with equal signed areas cancelling. The total distance travelled, $\int_0^3 |v(t)|\, dt$, is strictly positive and requires splitting the integral at the zero $t = 2$.

Key Theorem

(Fundamental Theorem, Part II) If $F$ is any antiderivative of $v$ on $[a, b]$, then

$$\int_a^b v(t)\, dt = F(b) - F(a).$$

This theorem transforms the infinite Riemann-sum limit into a finite algebraic evaluation, making displacement computable from a position function alone.

Lab Cell (SymPy)

from sympy import symbols, integrate, Abs

t = symbols('t')
v = 3*t**2 - 6*t

displacement    = integrate(v, (t, 0, 3))
total_distance  = integrate(Abs(v), (t, 0, 3))

print("Displacement  :", displacement)   # 0
print("Total distance:", total_distance) # 8

Invitation

Every domain that tracks change through time — from the trajectory of a charged particle in an electromagnetic field to the net cash flow of a financial instrument — rests on exactly this integral. Choose the application closest to your own curiosity and carry the argument one step further: replace the constant limits with variable endpoints, write $x(t) = x_0 + \int_{t_0}^t v(s)\, ds$, and you will have reconstructed the position function in full generality. That single step opens the door to differential equations, and to the rest of mathematical science.
