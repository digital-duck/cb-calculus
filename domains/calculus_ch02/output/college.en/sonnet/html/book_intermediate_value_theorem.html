Concept Book: Intermediate Value Theorem

Payoff

Every concept in this book has built toward a single question: when can you guarantee that a solution exists? The Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT) is the answer for continuous functions on a closed interval, and it is the natural capstone because it transforms "I think there's a solution somewhere" into a rigorous existence proof — without ever finding the solution explicitly.

The theorem. If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $N$ is any value strictly between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that $f(c) = N$. In the special case $N = 0$, this is the root-location corollary: if $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs, $f$ has a root in $(a, b)$.

Worked example. Show that $f(x) = x^5 - 3x + 1$ has a root in $[1, 2]$.

Evaluate at the endpoints: $f(1) = 1 - 3 + 1 = -1 < 0$ and $f(2) = 32 - 6 + 1 = 27 > 0$. Because $f$ is a polynomial (hence continuous everywhere), and $f(1) < 0 < f(2)$, the IVT guarantees a $c \in (1, 2)$ where $f(c) = 0$. The theorem says nothing about which $c$ or how many — only that one must exist.

Problem-solving application. The real power of IVT is that it is the engine behind bisection search, one of the most reliable root-finding algorithms in numerical computing. Because IVT guarantees a root whenever $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs, you can repeatedly halve the interval: check the midpoint $m = (a+b)/2$, determine which half still has a sign change, and recurse. Each iteration narrows the interval by half, so after $n$ steps the root is localized to within $(b - a)/2^n$. This connects pure existence reasoning — the IVT — to a concrete, programmable procedure.

The theorem also underlies fixed-point arguments (showing $g(x) = x$ has a solution by applying IVT to $h(x) = g(x) - x$), temperature and altitude problems in physics, and any engineering scenario where you need to certify that a parameter crosses a threshold without exhaustive search.

If you want to go deeper, follow the bisection thread: implement it for a polynomial of your choice, count the iterations needed to reach six decimal places of accuracy, and ask why the algorithm cannot fail as long as the IVT hypothesis holds. That single question will take you from pure mathematics into numerical analysis, error bounds, and the foundations of scientific computing.
