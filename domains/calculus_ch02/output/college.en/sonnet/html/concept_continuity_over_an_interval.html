Continuity Over An Interval

A function can be continuous at a single point without being well-behaved everywhere. The more useful idea is continuity over an interval — a guarantee that a function has no breaks, jumps, or holes across an entire stretch of its domain.

Definition. A function $f$ is continuous on an open interval $(a, b)$ if it is continuous at every point $c \in (a, b)$, meaning

$$\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$$

for each such $c$. For a closed interval $[a, b]$, two extra conditions are added at the endpoints: $f$ must be right-continuous at $a$ (i.e., $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = f(a)$) and left-continuous at $b$ (i.e., $\lim_{x \to b^-} f(x) = f(b)$). This prevents the function from "flying off" at either boundary.

Why it matters. Continuity on a closed interval unlocks two major theorems used constantly in calculus and analysis:

  • Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT): If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $k$ is any value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ with $f(c) = k$.
  • Extreme Value Theorem (EVT): If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, it must attain both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on that interval.

    • Worked example. Is $f(x) = \sqrt{4 - x^2}$ continuous on $[-2, 2]$?

    On the open interior $(-2, 2)$, the expression under the radical is strictly positive, so $f$ is defined and continuous everywhere there. At $x = -2$: $\lim_{x \to -2^+} \sqrt{4 - x^2} = 0 = f(-2)$ ✓. At $x = 2$: $\lim_{x \to 2^-} \sqrt{4-x^2} = 0 = f(2)$ ✓. So $f$ is continuous on $[-2, 2]$.

    Problem-solving application. Suppose you know $g(0) = -3$ and $g(5) = 7$, and $g$ is continuous on $[0, 5]$. Does $g$ ever equal zero? By the IVT, yes — since $0$ lies between $-3$ and $7$, there must be some $c \in (0, 5)$ where $g(c) = 0$. This is the foundation of root-finding algorithms like bisection search: the IVT tells you a root exists; repeated halving of the interval narrows down where it is. Without confirmed continuity on the full interval, neither conclusion holds.
