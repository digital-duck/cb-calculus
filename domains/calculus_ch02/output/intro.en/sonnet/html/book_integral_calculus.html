What Integral Calculus Achieves

Integral calculus is the mathematical discipline concerned with accumulation: given a rate of change, it recovers the total quantity that has accumulated. More precisely, if $f : [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is a continuous function, the definite integral

$$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k^*)\, \Delta x$$

assigns a real number to the area bounded by the curve $y = f(x)$ and the horizontal axis over $[a,b]$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, this limit is computed exactly through antidifferentiation: if $F' = f$, then $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$. This result is not merely a computational convenience; it reveals that differentiation and integration are inverse operations, unifying the two pillars of single-variable calculus into one coherent theory.

Worked Example

A particle moves along a line with velocity $v(t) = 3t^2 - 4t + 1$ m/s. The displacement over $t \in [0, 2]$ is

$$\int_0^2 (3t^2 - 4t + 1)\, dt = \left[t^3 - 2t^2 + t\right]_0^2 = (8 - 8 + 2) - 0 = 2 \text{ m.}$$

Even when $v(t)$ changes sign — meaning the particle reverses direction — the integral correctly accounts for net displacement, while $\int_0^2 |v(t)|\, dt$ yields the total distance traveled.

Key Theorem

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Part II). Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and let $F$ be any antiderivative of $f$. Then $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = F(b) - F(a)$.

The proof follows from the Mean Value Theorem applied to each subinterval of a Riemann partition; as the mesh tends to zero, the telescoping sum collapses to $F(b) - F(a)$.

Lab Cell (SymPy)

from sympy import symbols, integrate, Rational

t = symbols('t')
v = 3*t**2 - 4*t + 1
displacement = integrate(v, (t, 0, 2))
print(displacement)          # 2

Gateway to Multivariable Calculus

Integral calculus over a single variable is the foundation on which the entire architecture of multivariable calculus rests. The definite integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ generalises to the double integral $\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA$ over a planar region $D$, to the triple integral over a solid body, and ultimately to line and surface integrals that measure flux through curved manifolds. Each generalisation inherits the same core logic — partition, approximate, pass to the limit — while introducing the geometric richness of higher-dimensional domains.
