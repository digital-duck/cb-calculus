Concept Book: Multivariable Calculus

Payoff

Multivariable calculus is the mathematical language of the physical world. Every system of genuine complexity depends on more than one quantity: the temperature of an atmosphere varies with latitude, longitude, and altitude; the profit of a firm depends on price, quantity, and cost; the probability density of a quantum particle is a function of three spatial coordinates and time. Single-variable calculus equips the student to reason about change along a line; multivariable calculus extends that power to surfaces, volumes, and fields—to the full geometry of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

What the machinery achieves. The central objects are the partial derivative $\partial f/\partial x_i$, which isolates the rate of change in one direction while holding all others fixed, and the gradient $\nabla f = \left(\partial f/\partial x_1, \ldots, \partial f/\partial x_n\right)$, which encodes the direction and magnitude of steepest ascent. From these, one constructs the Jacobian matrix of a vector-valued map, the Hessian matrix of second-order curvature, and the iterated integrals that measure mass, flux, and probability over regions of arbitrary shape. The change-of-variables theorem—expressed through the absolute Jacobian determinant $|\det J|$—makes these integrals tractable across coordinate systems tuned to the geometry of each problem.

A concrete illustration. Let $f(x,y) = x^2 + xy + y^2$. Setting $\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$ gives the system $2x + y = 0$, $x + 2y = 0$, whose unique solution is $(0,0)$. The Hessian at the origin is

$$H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix},$$

with eigenvalues $1$ and $3$, both positive, confirming a strict local minimum. One computation—gradient, then Hessian, then eigenvalue sign—answers a question that would require pages of indirect argument in one variable.

The fundamental theorem. Stokes' theorem unifies the entire subject: for an oriented manifold $M$ with boundary $\partial M$ and a differential form $\omega$,

$$\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega.$$

Every classical result—Green's theorem, the divergence theorem, the fundamental theorem of calculus itself—is a special case. This single statement is why multivariable calculus is the natural capstone: it shows that differentiation and integration are not separate disciplines but dual faces of one geometric truth.

import sympy as sp
x, y = sp.symbols('x y')
f = x**2 + x*y + y**2
grad_f = [sp.diff(f, v) for v in (x, y)]
critical = sp.solve(grad_f, [x, y])
H = sp.hessian(f, [x, y])
print("Critical point:", critical)
print("Hessian:\n", H)
print("Eigenvalues:", H.eigenvals())
