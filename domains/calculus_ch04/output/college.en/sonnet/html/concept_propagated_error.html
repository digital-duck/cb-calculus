Propagated Error

When you measure a physical quantity, you always carry some uncertainty—a ruler might be precise to ±1 mm, a stopwatch to ±0.01 s. The trouble begins when you combine measurements in a formula. Each individual uncertainty bleeds into the final result, and the total uncertainty in that result is called the propagated error (or propagation of uncertainty).

The core rule: if a quantity $Q$ depends on independently measured variables $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, each with uncertainty $\delta x_i$, then the uncertainty in $Q$ is

$$\delta Q = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x_1}\delta x_1\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x_2}\delta x_2\right)^2 + \cdots + \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x_n}\delta x_n\right)^2}$$

Each partial derivative captures how sensitive $Q$ is to a small change in that variable. The square-root-of-sum-of-squares form assumes the errors are independent and random; it prevents worst-case pessimism from simply adding all errors together.

Worked example. You measure a rectangular table to find its area $A = L \times W$. You get $L = 2.00 \pm 0.02\ \text{m}$ and $W = 1.50 \pm 0.01\ \text{m}$.

The partial derivatives are $\frac{\partial A}{\partial L} = W = 1.50$ and $\frac{\partial A}{\partial W} = L = 2.00$. Plugging in:

$$\delta A = \sqrt{(1.50 \times 0.02)^2 + (2.00 \times 0.01)^2} = \sqrt{(0.030)^2 + (0.020)^2} = \sqrt{0.0013} \approx 0.036\ \text{m}^2$$

So the area is $A = 3.00 \pm 0.04\ \text{m}^2$ (rounded to one significant figure in the uncertainty).

Problem-solving application. A practical shortcut for products and quotients: the relative uncertainty in $Q = x^a y^b$ is

$$\frac{\delta Q}{Q} = \sqrt{\left(a\frac{\delta x}{x}\right)^2 + \left(b\frac{\delta y}{y}\right)^2}$$

Suppose you compute kinetic energy $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ with $m = 5.0 \pm 0.1\ \text{kg}$ and $v = 10.0 \pm 0.3\ \text{m/s}$. The relative uncertainties are $\frac{0.1}{5.0} = 2\%$ for mass and $2 \times \frac{0.3}{10.0} = 6\%$ for velocity (the exponent 2 doubles $v$'s contribution). Combined: $\sqrt{(2\%)^2 + (6\%)^2} \approx 6.3\%$. With $K = 250\ \text{J}$, the propagated error is about $\pm 16\ \text{J}$.

The key insight: variables that appear with higher powers—or that already carry large relative uncertainties—dominate the final error budget. Identifying that dominant term tells you exactly where to invest effort in taking more careful measurements.
