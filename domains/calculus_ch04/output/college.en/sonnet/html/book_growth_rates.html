Concept Book: Growth Rates

Calculus Ch04 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every quantitative field eventually asks the same question in different clothing: how fast is this changing, and does that rate itself change? Growth rates are the answer. A growth rate is not merely the difference between two values — it is the ratio of change to current size, often expressed per unit time, which makes it comparable across scales, currencies, populations, and processors alike.

The concept earns its place as a capstone because it unifies nearly every analytical tool built in this course. Derivatives measure instantaneous growth rates. Logarithms linearize exponential growth so that a straight line on a log-scale plot immediately reveals whether a quantity is growing geometrically. Big-O notation ranks algorithms by their asymptotic growth rate, making it possible to predict runtime before a single line of code runs on a real machine.

Consider a straightforward worked example. Suppose a city's population is $P(t) = 500{,}000 \cdot e^{0.03t}$, where $t$ is years from today. The absolute change in a single year near $t = 0$ is roughly $15{,}000$ people. Ten years later the same 3 % rate produces nearly $20{,}000$ new residents per year — same rate, larger absolute impact. The growth rate $r = 0.03$ is the invariant; the derivative $P'(t) = r \cdot P(t)$ shows exactly why the absolute numbers keep climbing even when nothing about the underlying dynamics has changed.

That insight scales directly into problem-solving. Comparing two investment strategies? Compute their annualized growth rates and subtract — compounding does the rest. Choosing between a sorting algorithm that scales as $O(n \log n)$ versus $O(n^2)$? Growth rates tell you the crossover point where one overtakes the other, and by how much at production scale. Modeling viral spread or battery discharge or drug clearance? Each is a differential equation whose solution is determined entirely by its growth (or decay) rate constant.

The applications are not separate topics bolted on after the theory — they are the same idea wearing different domain clothes. A compound-interest formula, a population logistic curve, and a Big-O bound are all answers to how fast, relative to how much you already have?

From here, the most rewarding path forward is to pick one domain and go deep. If you are drawn to computer science, explore how amortized analysis uses growth rates to explain why dynamic arrays are fast on average even when individual insertions are slow. If biology or public health pulls you, model an SIR epidemic and watch how a small change in the transmission rate $\beta$ shifts the entire trajectory of an outbreak. Either path will show you that mastering growth rates does not end an inquiry — it opens one.
