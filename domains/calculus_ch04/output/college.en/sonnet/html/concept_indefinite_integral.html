Indefinite Integral

An indefinite integral is the reverse operation of differentiation. If $F'(x) = f(x)$, then $F$ is called an antiderivative of $f$, and the indefinite integral is written

$$\int f(x)\, dx = F(x) + C$$

where $C$ is an arbitrary constant called the constant of integration. The $+C$ is not a formality — it reflects the fact that infinitely many functions share the same derivative. For example, $x^2$, $x^2 + 5$, and $x^2 - 17$ all differentiate to $2x$, so $\int 2x\, dx = x^2 + C$ captures the entire family at once.

The foundational rule for polynomials is the power rule for antiderivatives: for any $n \neq -1$,

$$\int x^n\, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$$

This is the direct mirror of the differentiation power rule and handles the majority of introductory problems.

Worked example. Find $\displaystyle\int (3x^2 - 4x + 7)\, dx$.

Integration is linear, so you can integrate term by term:

$$\int 3x^2\, dx = 3 \cdot \frac{x^3}{3} = x^3, \qquad \int 4x\, dx = 4 \cdot \frac{x^2}{2} = 2x^2, \qquad \int 7\, dx = 7x$$

Combining with a single constant of integration:

$$\int (3x^2 - 4x + 7)\, dx = x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x + C$$

Always verify by differentiating the result: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x + C) = 3x^2 - 4x + 7$. ✓

Problem-solving application. The indefinite integral becomes fully useful when paired with an initial condition. Suppose a particle has velocity $v(t) = 6t^2 - 10t$ m/s and starts at position $s(0) = 4$ m. Find $s(t)$.

First, integrate velocity to get the position family:

$$s(t) = \int (6t^2 - 10t)\, dt = 2t^3 - 5t^2 + C$$

Then substitute the initial condition to pin down $C$:

$$s(0) = 2(0)^3 - 5(0)^2 + C = 4 \implies C = 4$$

So $s(t) = 2t^3 - 5t^2 + 4$.

This two-step pattern — integrate to get the general family, then use one known value to select the specific solution — recurs throughout physics, engineering, and economics. The indefinite integral gives you the full solution space; the initial condition narrows it to the one that fits your situation.
