Concept Book: Propagated Error

Calculus Ch04 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every measurement you make carries uncertainty. A ruler reads to the nearest millimeter; a stopwatch to the nearest hundredth of a second; a digital thermometer to the nearest tenth of a degree. On their own, these uncertainties feel minor. The problem is that real calculations chain measurements together—area from two length readings, speed from distance divided by time, density from mass divided by volume. Each step in that chain can amplify or suppress the uncertainties from the steps before it. Propagated error is the discipline of tracking that amplification honestly, so that a final numerical result arrives with a defensible statement of its own precision.

The core machinery is compact. If a quantity $z$ depends on independent measured variables $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, and each $x_i$ carries an absolute uncertainty $\delta x_i$, then the propagated uncertainty in $z$ is

$$\delta z = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x_i}\right)^2 (\delta x_i)^2}$$

The partial derivative $\partial z / \partial x_i$ is the sensitivity coefficient for variable $i$: it tells you how hard a small error in $x_i$ pushes on the result. Squaring and summing under a root reflects the assumption that independent random errors tend to partially cancel rather than pile up worst-case.

Consider a concrete lab situation. You measure a cylinder's radius as $r = 3.0 \pm 0.1\ \text{cm}$ and its height as $h = 5.0 \pm 0.2\ \text{cm}$. The volume is $V = \pi r^2 h$. Applying the formula:

$$\delta V = \sqrt{\left(2\pi r h\,\delta r\right)^2 + \left(\pi r^2\,\delta h\right)^2}$$

Substituting gives $\delta V \approx 10\ \text{cm}^3$ on a nominal $V \approx 141\ \text{cm}^3$—roughly a 7% uncertainty. Without this calculation you might naively report $V = 141\ \text{cm}^3$ as if it were exact, which overstates your knowledge by an order of magnitude.

This principle reaches far beyond the physics lab. In numerical computing, propagated error explains why subtracting two nearly equal floating-point numbers catastrophically destroys significant digits—a phenomenon called catastrophic cancellation that topples otherwise stable algorithms. In engineering, sensitivity coefficients guide tolerance allocation: tighten the component whose $|\partial z/\partial x_i|$ is largest, and you buy the most precision per dollar of manufacturing cost. In data science and machine learning, uncertainty propagation underlies confidence intervals on model predictions, and in Bayesian inference it appears as the law of total variance, connecting prior uncertainty through a likelihood to posterior uncertainty. In surveying, navigation, and GPS, accumulated error budgets determine whether a position fix is trustworthy to a meter or only to a kilometer.

Each of these domains uses the same underlying logic—compute sensitivities, combine them quadratically, interpret the result—but applies it to a different kind of chain. If any one of these strikes you as worth pursuing, dive into catastrophic cancellation next: it takes the abstract formula and turns it into a concrete debugging skill you will use every time you write a numerical program.
