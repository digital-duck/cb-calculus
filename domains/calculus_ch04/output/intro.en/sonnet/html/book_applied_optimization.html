Concept Book: Applied Optimization

Payoff

Applied optimization is the discipline of finding, among all admissible choices, the one that makes a real-valued objective as large or as small as possible while respecting every constraint the problem imposes. Formally, we seek

$$x^* = \arg\min_{x \in \mathcal{F}} f(x),$$

where $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is the objective function and $\mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is the feasible set defined by equality and inequality constraints. This formulation is not a narrow technique; it is the lingua franca through which mathematics, science, and engineering speak to one another.

Worked example. A manufacturer produces two goods whose weekly profits (in dollars) are $3x_1 + 5x_2$. Production is constrained by two resources: $x_1 + 2x_2 \le 12$ and $2x_1 + x_2 \le 10$, with $x_1, x_2 \ge 0$. Graphing the feasible polygon and evaluating the objective at each vertex yields the maximum profit of $\$30$ at $(x_1, x_2) = (2, 5)$. The geometry here is no accident: the optimal solution lives on the boundary, a fact codified by the theory developed throughout this text.

Theorem (First-Order Necessary Conditions). Let $f$ and the active constraint functions be continuously differentiable at $x^$. If $x^$ is a local minimizer and a regularity condition (e.g., the LICQ) holds, then there exist multipliers $\lambda_i \ge 0$ and $\mu_j$ such that

$$\nabla f(x^*) + \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i(x^*) + \sum_j \mu_j \nabla h_j(x^*) = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \lambda_i g_i(x^*) = 0 \;\forall i.$$

These Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions are the capstone identity of the entire course: every concept encountered—gradients, convexity, duality, feasibility—converges here into a single, testable certificate of optimality.

Lab cell.

from sympy import symbols, Rational, solve, lambdify
import sympy as sp

x1, x2, l1, l2 = symbols('x1 x2 lambda1 lambda2', nonneg=True)
f  = -(3*x1 + 5*x2)                      # negate for minimization
g1 = x1 + 2*x2 - 12
g2 = 2*x1 + x2 - 10

stationarity = [sp.diff(f, x1) + l1*sp.diff(g1, x1) + l2*sp.diff(g2, x1),
                sp.diff(f, x2) + l1*sp.diff(g1, x2) + l2*sp.diff(g2, x2)]

sol = solve(stationarity + [g1, g2], [x1, x2, l1, l2])
print(sol)   # expects x1=2, x2=5, lambda1=Rational(7,3), lambda2=Rational(1,3)
