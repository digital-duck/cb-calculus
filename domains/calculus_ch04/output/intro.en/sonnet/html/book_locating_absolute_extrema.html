Concept Book: Locating Absolute Extrema

Calculus Ch04 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Definition. The absolute extrema of a function $f$ on a closed interval $[a, b]$ are the values $f(c_{\min})$ and $f(c_{\max})$ such that

$$f(c_{\min}) \leq f(x) \leq f(c_{\max}) \quad \text{for all } x \in [a, b].$$

The procedure for locating these values — evaluating $f$ at every critical point in $(a, b)$ and at both endpoints, then comparing — is called the Closed Interval Method. It is not a heuristic: it is an exhaustive search over the only candidates the theory allows.

This finality is what makes the concept a natural endpoint. Every technique developed earlier in this text — limits, continuity, differentiation, the Mean Value Theorem — was aimed, implicitly, at answering the same question: where does a quantity achieve its best or worst value? Locating absolute extrema is the moment those tools converge into a single, decisive algorithm.

Worked Example. A manufacturer produces $x$ units at daily cost $C(x) = 0.02x^2 - 4x + 500$ dollars, with the production constraint $x \in [50, 150]$. Setting $C'(x) = 0.04x - 4 = 0$ gives the critical point $x = 100$. Evaluating at all candidates:

$$C(50) = 350, \quad C(100) = 300, \quad C(150) = 350.$$

The absolute minimum cost is $\$300$, achieved at $x = 100$ units. No calculus shortcut could have established this with confidence without also checking the endpoints.

Theorem (Extreme Value Theorem). If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, then $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on $[a, b]$. Existence is guaranteed; the Closed Interval Method locates them.

The theorem's proof relies on the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ and the compactness of $[a, b]$: every open cover has a finite subcover, so $f$ cannot "escape" to an unrealized supremum.

from sympy import symbols, diff, solve, Rational

x = symbols('x')
C = Rational(2, 100)*x**2 - 4*x + 500
candidates = solve(diff(C, x), x) + [50, 150]
min_cost = min((C.subs(x, c), c) for c in candidates)
print(min_cost)   # (300, 100)

Connection to Applied Optimization. In applied optimization, every real-world objective — minimizing material cost, maximizing structural load, finding the most efficient dosage interval — reduces to precisely this problem: locate the absolute extremum of a model function on a feasible domain. The domain may be multidimensional, the constraints may be inequalities, and the function may arrive from data rather than a formula, but the logical core is unchanged.
