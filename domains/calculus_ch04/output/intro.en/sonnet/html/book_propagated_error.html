Concept Book: Propagated Error

Calculus Ch04 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Throughout this text we have built the machinery of symbolic differentiation, limit definitions, and Taylor approximation not as ends in themselves but as instruments aimed at a single practical question: when the inputs to a calculation carry uncertainty, how much uncertainty propagates into the output? The concept of propagated error is the answer to that question made precise, and it is the natural culmination of every tool we have assembled.

Definition. Let $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable at a point $\mathbf{x}_0 = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$. If each input $x_i$ is known only up to an absolute uncertainty $\delta x_i > 0$, the first-order propagated error in $f$ is

$$\delta f \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\bigg|_{\mathbf{x}_0}\right| \delta x_i.$$

This bound follows directly from the triangle inequality applied to the first-order Taylor expansion of $f$; it is conservative but tight when the partial derivatives do not cancel.

Worked example. The kinetic energy of a projectile is $K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. Suppose $m = 2.00 \pm 0.02\,\text{kg}$ and $v = 10.0 \pm 0.1\,\text{m s}^{-1}$. The partial derivatives are $\partial K/\partial m = \tfrac{1}{2}v^2 = 50$ and $\partial K/\partial v = mv = 20$, so

$$\delta K = 50 \cdot (0.02) + 20 \cdot (0.1) = 1.0 + 2.0 = 3.0\,\text{J}.$$

The nominal value is $K = 100\,\text{J}$, giving a relative uncertainty of $3\%$. Notice that the velocity term dominates despite its smaller absolute uncertainty, a fact invisible without this analysis.

Key theorem (quadrature propagation). When the input errors $\delta x_i$ are independent and random with standard deviations $\sigma_i$, the standard deviation of $f$ satisfies

$$\sigma_f \;=\; \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right)^2 \sigma_i^2},$$

a result known as the law of propagation of uncertainty (JCGM 100:2008). The quadrature form is strictly smaller than the worst-case sum, reflecting the statistical near-impossibility that all errors align.

Why this is the natural endpoint. Every prior chapter contributed one ingredient: symbolic differentiation supplies the partial derivatives; Taylor's theorem justifies the linear approximation; and the norm inequalities convert a local linear bound into a global uncertainty certificate. Propagated error is the synthesis—a single number that quantifies how faithfully a model speaks about the world given imperfect measurements.

Lab cell.

from sympy import symbols, sqrt, Abs, diff, latex

m, v, dm, dv = symbols('m v delta_m delta_v', positive=True)
K = m * v**2 / 2

# Worst-case propagated error
delta_K = Abs(diff(K, m)) * dm + Abs(diff(K, v)) * dv
print(latex(delta_K))

# Evaluate numerically
print(delta_K.subs({m: 2, v: 10, dm: 0.02, dv: 0.1}))

Running this cell reproduces the worked example and invites immediate experimentation: change the functional form, add a third variable, or switch to the quadrature formula and observe how the bound tightens.

The machinery you now hold is not specific to mechanics. Geodesists use it to certify GPS coordinates; pharmacologists use it to bound drug-dosage error; financial engineers use it to price the sensitivity of derivative contracts. Each domain reinterprets $\delta x_i$ in its own units, but the gradient structure is universal. Choose the application closest to your interests and carry the analysis one level deeper—the next chapter begins exactly there.
